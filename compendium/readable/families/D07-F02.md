## Central question

What information is certified by local analytic data, and which global conclusions require a domain or positivity theorem?

## Synopsis

Analytic reasoning becomes especially effective when its domain is part of the input. Cauchy integrals extract coefficients only when the disk and singularity assumptions hold. Logarithmic derivatives locate zeros and poles locally but also carry branch and continuation data. A contour argument counts zeros with multiplicity; it need not show that two counted zeros coincide. The 2026 phase and positive-atom discussions add a useful constructive class: a rational function built from positive real atoms has a fixed imaginary sign and strictly ordered real zeros. That model connects coefficient extraction, monotonicity and spectral measures without making any claim about an unspecified zeta operator. It also provides a small exact laboratory in which poles, residues, roots and signs can all be checked.

## Definitions

A holomorphic function is complex differentiable on an open set. Cauchy extraction on |z−a|=r requires holomorphy on a neighborhood of the closed disk.

A Herglotz function maps the upper half-plane into itself. For distinct real t_j and positive μ_j, M(z)=Σ_j μ_j/(t_j−z) is a rational example.

The argument principle counts zeros minus poles inside a contour that avoids both; the count includes multiplicity.

## Theorem

For t₁<⋯<t_m and μ_j>0, M(z)=Σ μ_j/(t_j−z) is Herglotz. On every interval (t_j,t_{j+1}) it is strictly increasing from −∞ to +∞ and therefore has exactly one simple zero. Its poles are simple with residues −μ_j.

## Proof

Write z=x+iy with y>0. The imaginary part of 1/(t−z) is y/((t−x)²+y²)>0; positive weighted summation proves the half-plane property. For real x away from the poles, M′(x)=Σ μ_j/(t_j−x)²>0. Immediately to the right of t_j its own term tends to −∞, and immediately to the left of t_{j+1} that pole contributes +∞. Continuity and the intermediate value theorem give a zero, strict monotonicity gives uniqueness, and a positive derivative at that zero makes it simple. Multiplying the jth term by z−t_j and taking the limit gives residue −μ_j. Outside the extreme poles all denominators have the same sign, so no further real zero occurs. The argument proves the entire finite interlacing structure without plotting a dense scatter of points. A difference of two such functions does not preserve positive weights and has to be reanalyzed. Separately, the contour integral f^(n)(a)=n!/(2πi)∮f(z)/(z−a)^(n+1)dz follows by the convergent Taylor expansion on a smaller disk and termwise integration; its size bound is n!M/r^n. These two exact mechanisms explain why sign, locality and the contour hypothesis should travel with any calculated residue or root count.

## Worked example: setup

Take M(z)=1/(−1−z)+2/(2−z), with atoms of masses one and two at −1 and 2.

## Worked example: calculation

Combining terms gives M(z)=−3z/[(-1−z)(2−z)]. The unique real zero between the poles is zero, M′(0)=1+2/4=3/2, and the residues are −1 and −2. The reciprocal slope at the zero is 2/3. By comparison, p(z)=z²−1 has contour count two on |z|=2 but two distinct simple roots; its count does not certify a double root.

## Worked example: meaning

The atom model supplies an exact positive spectral function and a root certificate. A phase formula may reproduce its local masses only after the chosen A/B convention and derivative sign are fixed.

## Limits

The finite positive-atom theorem requires distinct real poles and strictly positive masses. Limits of such functions need convergence hypotheses. Formal positivity of a kernel and pointwise positivity do not imply positive definiteness.

## Open questions
