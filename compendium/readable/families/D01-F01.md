## Central question

Which arithmetic statements survive a change of scalar domain, and what can a finite p-adic calculation actually certify?

## Synopsis

An arithmetic expression includes its scalar ring, topology and precision. The rational field, a p-adic completion, a residue ring and a formal power-series ring support different operations. A unital homomorphism between characteristic zero and characteristic p would send the nonzero integer p both to an invertible element and to zero, an immediate obstruction when the source is a field. Completion instead preserves rational identities while changing which sequences converge. These distinctions turn the early request to separate meanings of Q into a practical specification for every later lifting algorithm. The useful output is a compatible sequence of exact congruences accompanied by derivative and residual valuations. Such a sequence proves an existence statement only with a convergence argument; a zero residue at one precision cannot certify an exact zero. Perfectoid and prismatic language extends this discipline, but carries additional structural hypotheses rather than merely renaming small errors.

## Definitions

For nonzero rational x, v_p(x) is its prime-p exponent and |x|_p=p^(−v_p(x)); Q_p is the metric completion and Z_p its unit ball.

A simple root modulo p is an integer a with f(a)≡0 mod p and f′(a) not divisible by p. A precision-N representative specifies a coset modulo p^N.

For a nonzero integral series F=Σa_nT^n, μ(F)=min v_p(a_n), and λ(F) is the first index attaining that minimum. Infinite tails must be controlled before certifying these minima.

## Theorem

If f∈Z_p[X] and a_n is a root modulo p^n with unit derivative, there is exactly one a_(n+1)=a_n+t p^n modulo p^(n+1) reducing to a_n. The compatible sequence converges to the unique root of f in a_1+pZ_p.

## Proof

Taylor expansion modulo p^(n+1) gives f(a_n+t p^n)≡f(a_n)+t p^n f′(a_n), since all terms of order two are divisible by p^(2n). Dividing by p^n gives one linear equation for t modulo p. The derivative is a unit, so t is unique. Successive representatives differ by an element of p^nZ_p and hence form a Cauchy sequence. Completeness supplies a limit a; continuity of polynomial evaluation gives f(a)=0. If b is a second root in the same residue class, factor f(a)−f(b)=(a−b)g(a,b). The reduction of g is the nonzero derivative at a_1, so g is a unit and a=b. Newton's update a−f(a)/f′(a) accelerates the same argument: an initial residual valuation at least n becomes at least 2n when the derivative is a unit. An exact vanishing claim requires this theorem or a separate identity, whereas finite arithmetic alone establishes only congruences.

## Limits

Simple-root lifting does not cover a vanishing derivative without stronger hypotheses. Truncated series cannot reveal unseen unit coefficients. Perfectoid and prismatic constructions require their full structural definitions; a numerical resemblance to a weight or height supplies none of them.

## Open questions

For a singular residue, what extra valuation inequalities guarantee a lift, and can an exact counterexample show why the simple-root contract fails?

Can a proposed filtered calculation provide a certified lower bound on input precision sufficient to distinguish nonvanishing from an unresolved zero residue?
