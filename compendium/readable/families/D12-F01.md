## Central question

What finite certificate proves global optimality, including null directions and boundary cases?

## Synopsis

The optimization strand is strongest when an answer arrives with a lower bound and a feasible point attaining it. The source sequence links constrained covariance minimization, Fenchel conjugates, polyhedral actions and semidefinite primal-dual gaps. These are several forms of one principle: weak duality gives an inequality valid over the entire feasible set, and a matching construction turns it into a global theorem. The singular cases deserve equal status with the invertible ones. A pseudoinverse is meaningful only after the nullspace has been checked against the constraint; stationarity alone cannot provide the missing boundedness or convexity.

## Definitions

For convex f, its conjugate is f*(p)=sup_x(p·x−f(x)); the domain of x is part of f and changes the conjugate.

A symmetric covariance matrix Σ≥0 defines the objective wᵀΣw. The affine budget is cᵀw=1 with c≠0.

The Moore–Penrose inverse Σ⁺ inverts positive eigenspaces and vanishes on the nullspace.

An SDP primal feasible X≥0 and dual slack S=C−Σ_i y_iA_i≥0 satisfy constraints Tr(A_iX)=b_i.

## Theorem

If some z∈kerΣ has cᵀz≠0, the constrained quadratic minimum is zero. Otherwise c∈rangeΣ and, with q=cᵀΣ⁺c>0, all minimizers are Σ⁺c/q+z for z∈kerΣ, with minimum 1/q. For every SDP feasible pair, the objective gap is Tr(SX)≥0; a zero gap certifies global optimality without requiring a separate strong-duality theorem.

## Proof

In the first quadratic case scale z by 1/(cᵀz); feasibility and zero energy follow, while nonnegativity proves minimality. Otherwise decompose w into positive spectral and null components. The constraint sees only the positive part. Cauchy–Schwarz applied to Σ^{1/2}w and Σ^{+1/2}c gives 1=(cᵀw)²≤(wᵀΣw)(cᵀΣ⁺c), so the objective is at least 1/q. Equality gives the displayed positive component, with arbitrary invisible null addition. For the SDP, subtract the objectives, insert the primal equalities, and obtain Tr(SX). This is nonnegative because Tr(SX)=Tr(X^{1/2}SX^{1/2}) and the enclosed matrix is PSD. A feasible zero-gap pair therefore sandwiches the optimum exactly.

## Worked example: setup

Minimize w_1²+4w_2² subject to w_1+w_2=1. Compare Σ=diag(0,4), then Σ=11ᵀ. A separate SDP minimizes Tr(diag(1,2)X) subject to Tr X=1 and X≥0.

## Worked example: calculation

For the positive definite quadratic, q=1+1/4=5/4 and w*=(4/5,1/5), giving value 4/5. For diag(0,4), the null vector e_1 satisfies the budget and achieves zero. For 11ᵀ, every feasible point has value one; the nullspace is budget-invisible. In the SDP, X*=diag(1,0), y=1 and S=diag(0,1) are feasible and give Tr(SX*)=0. Finally, the conjugate of kx² on all real x is p²/(4k), but restricting x≥0 changes it to max(p,0)²/(4k).

## Worked example: meaning

The same computational discipline applies across these examples: inspect feasibility, preserve the actual domain, identify nullspaces, and produce a global inequality. A solver residual can guide a candidate, while an exact rational primal-dual pair can turn it into a reviewable certificate. Boundary constraints may alter the optimum and cannot be repaired by postprocessing an unconstrained answer.

## Limits

KKT equations are sufficient only under the appropriate convexity and regularity conditions; a cubic stationary system may contain saddles or fail to have a global maximizer. Zero-gap feasible certificates avoid an appeal to strong duality but do require exact feasibility. Floating-point near-feasibility needs explicit error bounds before it becomes a certificate. A convex relaxation need not attain the original discrete optimum.
