## Central question

What boundary state is sufficient to compose local computations without changing a count or optimum?

## Synopsis

A large combinatorial computation is often tractable because a separator carries only the information that the unprocessed part can still observe. The mathematics is distributivity plus a correct boundary state, rather than a promise that every graph is sparse. Sum–product elimination computes counts or partition sums; min–plus elimination computes minimum additive costs. Both depend on the same factor scopes, but they answer different questions and require different identities. An auxiliary gate with a unique extension preserves the number of original objects; an arbitrary satisfiability-preserving encoding may not. A channel quotient is legitimate only if forgotten lane identities cannot affect future feasibility or cost. These are not independent implementation details: they specify the exact relation between a compact state and the population or value it represents. The useful design process proves a local elimination identity, proves the quotient lift condition and retains enough separator data to make every future continuation well defined.

## Definitions

A factor model over a commutative semiring has Z=⊕_x ⊗_j f_j(x_scope(j)). Ordinary nonnegative integers give counting; min–plus uses min as addition and numerical + as multiplication.

Eliminating a variable v produces a new factor on its surviving neighboring scope: g(y)=⊕_v ⊗_{j:v∈scope(j)}f_j.

A separator state stores the values or equivalence classes through which processed and unprocessed factors can interact. Its adequacy is a statement about every compatible continuation.

For a relation F(x,a), the auxiliary fibre m(x)=#{a:F(x,a)} measures whether projecting auxiliaries preserves a count.

## Theorem

Finite semiring elimination preserves Z by distributivity, in any elimination order. Over counting integers, an encoding preserves #P exactly when its satisfying auxiliary fibre is one over each x∈P and zero otherwise. If a quotient map is surjective onto feasible quotient states and feasibility and objective are constant on its fibres, the original and quotient optimum values are equal.

## Proof

Separate the factors involving v from the others. The independent factors can be taken outside ⊕_v, and the remaining expression is g by definition. Repetition proves exact elimination; changing order changes intermediate scopes but not the final algebraic value. Over integers, the number of satisfying pairs is Σ_x m(x), so unique extension gives #F=#P; mere existential equivalence does not control m(x). For optimization, every original object projects to a quotient object with equal cost, so the quotient infimum is no larger. Every feasible quotient object has a feasible lift with that cost, proving the reverse inequality. Future operations descend only when they preserve fibres as well, which is a stronger requirement than preserving the present objective.

## Worked example: setup

Introduce a=x₁ XOR x₂, then erase lane identities from four labeled objects injected into sixteen channels arranged as four banks of four.

## Worked example: calculation

The XOR gate has one auxiliary extension for each of four original inputs; adding a=1 leaves exactly 01 and 10. An unconstrained auxiliary would double the count to eight. In the channel model there are 16·15·14·13=43,680 physical assignments and 4^4=256 bank tuples. A tuple with bank occupancies n_b has ∏_b P(4,n_b) lifts. The all-in-one-bank tuple has 24 lifts; the four-distinct-banks tuple has 256. Summing all fibre sizes returns 43,680. The full quotient prefix tree has 1+4+16+64+256=341 nodes. Thus uniform quotient sampling is not uniform physical sampling. On a binary path, a min–plus message m_{i+1}(t)=min_s[m_i(s)+φ_i(s,t)] retains the boundary bit; collapsing two boundary bits is legal only when all future costs agree after the collapse.

## Worked example: meaning

The separator is a mathematical interface: exact elimination, optimization and quotient sampling each require the interface to retain the information relevant to that task.

## Limits

Intermediate factor size depends on elimination width; distributivity does not imply polynomial complexity. Semiring choice changes the output. Auxiliary existential projection preserves satisfiability more weakly than counts. The original routing graph, byte-hop objective and claimed 9,765,632 optimum were not recovered, so neither those values nor timing claims are certified here.
