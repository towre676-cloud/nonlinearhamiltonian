## Central question

Which feasible set and objective make a proposed improvement mathematically meaningful?

## Synopsis

Many optimization questions initially hide their most consequential choice: what is being optimized over which alternatives. Clustering weights can duplicate one scatter identity; capacity quotas are different from score weights; Pareto points can resist every linear selector; a QUBO is meaningful only after the binary variables and constraints are fixed. The later equivalence-class and separator constructions improve search by preserving the cost of every legal continuation. This family unifies these questions around a typed feasible set and a certified objective, while distinguishing an abstract optimum from a measured implementation cost.

## Definitions

A feasible optimization problem is (X,J), with optimum inf_{x∈X}J(x); changing X or J changes the problem.

A vector objective is Pareto nondominated when no feasible competitor improves all coordinates and at least one strictly.

A QUBO minimizes a quadratic polynomial on {0,1}^n; a graph-cut objective is one exact finite instance.

A frontier state in dynamic programming is sufficient only when its signature retains all information connecting processed choices to future feasibility and cost.

## Theorem

For fixed data, total scatter T equals within-cluster scatter W plus between-cluster scatter B. Therefore maximizing aB−bW with a,b≥0 and a+b>0 is equivalent to minimizing W over the same partitions. Separately, if a frontier signature determines every future interaction, retaining only the cheapest prefix per signature preserves the global optimum.

## Proof

For the scatter identity, insert x_i−μ=(x_i−μ_{c(i)})+(μ_{c(i)}−μ). Cross terms vanish within each cluster because Σ_{i∈c}(x_i−μ_c)=0. Hence T=W+B and aB−bW=aT−(a+b)W, differing from −W only by a positive scale and constant. For frontier compression, take two partial solutions with the same sufficient signature. Every legal completion contributes the same added cost and has the same feasibility for both; replacing the dearer prefix with the cheaper one can only improve the total. Induct over processed layers. The proof requires equality of continuation behavior, not merely equality of the currently best continuation or the present objective value.

## Worked example: setup

Use the triangle maximum-cut problem and a two-state repeated-layer transfer. Also consider minimization of two costs at feasible points A=(0,2), B=(1,3/2), C=(2,0).

## Worked example: calculation

The triangle has eight binary labelings: the two constant ones cut zero edges and all six nonconstant ones cut two, proving optimum two by enumeration. In min-plus algebra, M=[[∞,2],[3,∞]] has M⊗M=5I_minplus. Thus with initial costs (0,∞), the minimum terminal sequence is 0,2,5,7,10,… and d_{L+2}=d_L+5. In the Pareto fixture all three points are nondominated. For positive weights α,β, choosing B over A requires α≤β/2, while choosing B over C requires α≥3β/2, impossible. The middle Pareto point cannot be reached by a positive linear selector.

## Worked example: meaning

The scatter identity shows how a proposed extra criterion can collapse algebraically; the Pareto example shows how a single scalarization can omit legitimate tradeoffs; the transfer identity shows how finite search can be compressed without losing its optimum. Together they support a stronger design practice: simplify the objective symbolically, preserve deployment constraints, and certify both the search reduction and the final feasible candidate.

## Limits

The exact examples do not replay absent historical placement tables, graph instances or runtime benchmarks. A sufficient separator may need auxiliary connectivity state, and algebraic compression preserves only its stated future predicate. A quantum circuit formula for one edge is a finite calculation, not an asymptotic algorithmic advantage. Nonlinear objectives or changed deployment constraints require a new feasible-set argument.

## Open questions

For a supplied search system, prove that its frontier signature separates past and future, or exhibit a minimal counterexample.

Enumerate a finite Pareto set and compare which members can be selected by linear, constrained and nonlinear preferences.
