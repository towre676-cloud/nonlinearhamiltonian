## Central question

Which next observation resolves the target at minimum guaranteed cost, and when does additional data remain irrelevant?

## Synopsis

Observation and decision become a single constructive problem when the remaining states and available tests are finite and explicit. A test splits the current feasible set into outcome fibres. A target is resolved when it is constant on the surviving set, even if the full hidden state remains unknown. Positive test costs lead to an exact worst-case Bellman recurrence with matching lower and upper proofs. This is a rigorous version of choosing the cheapest distinguishing experiment, and it complements linear target-factorization: one asks which extra measurement destroys the harmful ambiguity. Correlated uncertainty adds a geometric version. An ellipsoid has exact target extrema, while a hidden-target conditional variance can remain positive even with noiseless recorded channels. More readings do not automatically remove uncertainty if they repeat the same direction or measure variables unrelated to the target’s hidden component. The archive’s decision examples become strongest when their uncertainty sets and terminal target criterion are stated together.

## Definitions

Let S be a finite feasible state set, τ:S→T a target, and e a deterministic experiment of positive cost c_e with outcome e(s). Its outcome fibre is S_{e,y}={s∈S:e(s)=y}.

A terminal set is one on which τ is constant. A useful test has every nonempty outcome fibre a proper subset of the current nonterminal set.

For correlated uncertainty E={μ+Σ^{1/2}z:||z||≤r}, Σ is positive definite and a linear target is aᵀx.

Worst-case decision cost, expected cost under a prior and statistical information gain are different objectives.

## Theorem

If the available tests separate every pair of states having different target values, the least worst-case cost is V(S)=0 on terminal sets and otherwise V(S)=min_e[c_e+max_{y:S_{e,y}≠∅}V(S_{e,y})], over useful tests. For the ellipsoid E, the exact linear-target range is aᵀμ±r√(aᵀΣa).

## Proof

Any decision tree chooses a first test e. An adversary can return the outcome whose remaining subtree has largest optimal cost, so every tree costs at least c_e+max_yV(S_{e,y}). Minimizing gives the lower bound. Conversely attach optimal subtrees to a minimizing test. Every nonempty fibre is smaller, so induction on |S| constructs a tree attaining that same value. Pairwise target separation guarantees that a nonterminal set has a splitting test; otherwise two different target values could never be distinguished. Positive cost lets us discard useless first tests. This proves existence and exact optimality. For the ellipsoid write aᵀx=aᵀμ+(Σ^{1/2}a)ᵀz. Cauchy–Schwarz gives the stated extrema, attained at z=±rΣ^{1/2}a/√(aᵀΣa) when a≠0. Correlation is retained through the quadratic form rather than discarded in independent coordinate bars.

## Worked example: setup

Identify one of states a,b,c. Test e₁ costs one and isolates a; test e₂ costs two and isolates b; a direct three-way test costs four. Separately use μ=0,Σ=[[1,ρ],[ρ,1]], target x₁+x₂.

## Worked example: calculation

Testing e₁ first costs one if the answer is a and otherwise requires e₂, for worst-case cost three. Starting with e₂ also has worst-case cost three, while the direct test costs four. Neither cheaper one-test partition resolves every state, so the Bellman lower bound matches three. If the target merely distinguishes a from {b,c}, e₁ alone costs one; state identification was an unnecessarily demanding task. For the ellipsoid target, the exact range is ±r√(2+2ρ). Thus positive and negative correlations have opposite effects on the sum, and treating the axes independently can conceal this difference. In the linear observation model Hx=(x₁,x₂), target x₃ remains unobservable regardless of how often the first two coordinates are read.

## Worked example: meaning

An experiment is valuable relative to the target and the current uncertainty set. The optimal policy comes with a lower-bound argument, not just an appealing test order.

## Limits

The finite theorem assumes deterministic tests and a complete state/test table. Noisy outcomes require posterior feasible sets and a robust terminal condition. Repeated samples of the same deficient geometry do not create new linear rank. A development-set optimum does not establish future prediction performance, causal benefit or hardware savings. Historical panel timings need their original datasets and evaluation protocol.

## Open questions

Which targets can be resolved without identifying the complete state in a supplied experiment menu?

How does one preserve a computable lower bound when observations are noisy, costs depend on history or uncertainty sets are continuous?
