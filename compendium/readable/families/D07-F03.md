## Central question

How can an integral be reduced to an exact constant while keeping convergence, branch and normalization information visible?

## Synopsis

The archive contains a sustained exact-integration strand: elementary logarithmic moments, sine quotients, Catalan and dilogarithm values, theta and modular transformations, Mellin central values, elliptic logarithms and a final finite-phase integral. These belong together as calculational methods rather than as one claimed transcendence theory. A useful reduction identifies an integration domain, a convergent expansion, the interchange theorem and a final finite or controlled infinite expression. The resulting number can then be checked independently by rational recurrences, symmetry or tail enclosures. The elementary Mellin moment below is a central reusable example. It supplies factorial weights exactly and explains why positive geometric expansions turn certain logarithmic integrals into zeta sums. More elaborate modular and elliptic examples retain additional transformation and branch information that this elementary calculation does not replace.

## Definitions

For a>0 and integer m≥0, the Mellin logarithmic moment is J_m(a)=∫₀¹x^(a−1)(−log x)^m dx.

An exact special-function identity includes its parameter region and branch convention. A numerical agreement at one parameter is a check, not the identity itself.

A period representation here means a specified convergent integral or analytic continuation; no algebraic independence claim follows from that name.

## Theorem

For real a>0 and integer m≥0, J_m(a)=m!/a^(m+1). For m≥1, ∫₀¹(−log x)^m/(1−x) dx=m!ζ(m+1). Truncating the latter positive series after n=N leaves a tail between zero and (m!/m)N^(−m).

## Proof

Set x=e^(−t). Then dx=−e^(−t)dt and x^(a−1)dx=−e^(−at)dt, so the first integral becomes ∫₀∞e^(−at)t^m dt. The substitution u=at factors out a^(−m−1). Integration by parts gives Γ(m+1)=mΓ(m), starting from Γ(1)=1; the boundary terms vanish because an exponential dominates any fixed power. This proves the first formula and convergence at both endpoints. For 0<x<1, 1/(1−x)=Σ_{k≥0}x^k. All summands multiplied by (−log x)^m are nonnegative, so monotone convergence justifies exchanging integral and sum without an unproved uniform bound near x=1. Each resulting moment is m!/(k+1)^(m+1). The series converges exactly for m≥1 in the nonnegative-integer range. The tail is m!Σ_{n>N}n^(−m−1), at most m!∫_N∞t^(−m−1)dt=(m!/m)N^(−m). The m=0 integral diverges logarithmically, showing why the endpoint restriction is mathematical content. Complex a or a noninteger logarithmic exponent can be handled only after choosing powers and checking a new convergence region. This proof is deliberately transparent enough that its finite partial sums and error bounds can be reproduced with rational arithmetic.

## Worked example: setup

Compute J₃(2) and the integral K=∫₀¹(−log x)/(1−x) dx.

## Worked example: calculation

J₃(2)=3!/2⁴=3/8 exactly. For K, the first four rational summands give 1+1/4+1/9+1/16=205/144. The remaining positive tail is at most 1/4, hence 205/144<K<241/144. Euler’s separately established ζ(2)=π²/6 identifies the exact constant, while the rational enclosure already checks the reduction without using that evaluation.

## Worked example: meaning

This is a reproducible reduction with an explicit stopping rule. The same workflow is appropriate for the archive’s accelerated arctangent, product-variable and finite-phase identities, with their own expansions and convergence arguments.
