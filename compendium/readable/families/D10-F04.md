## Central question

When does repeated averaging simplify a law, and which quantitative information survives the limit?

## Synopsis

Averaging, renormalization, heavy tails, multiplicative products and infinite-dimensional Gaussian models share a demand for explicit scaling. A Gaussian fixed point is a theorem about normalized sums with finite variance, not a universal fate of every noise source. Cauchy stability provides an exact countermodel, and multiplicative products become additive only after taking logarithms. The fractal measure construction adds a different kind of approximation: convergence follows from a direct coupling bound rather than from a central limit theorem. This family distinguishes exact finite identities, limiting distributions and numerical approximations, preserving the assumptions that make each useful.

## Definitions

For independent copies X_1,X_2 of a centered variable, the averaging renormalization is R(X)=(X_1+X_2)/√2.

The characteristic function is φ(t)=E e^{itX}; independent summation multiplies characteristic functions.

An α-stable fixed law for R_α=(X_1+X_2)/2^{1/α} has characteristic function exp(−c|t|^α) in the symmetric model.

A Gaussian series X=Σ√q_nZ_ne_n is H-valued in mean square when Σq_n<∞; cylindrical coordinate consistency alone does not guarantee H-valued samples.

## Theorem

If a centered nondegenerate finite-variance law is fixed under R_α, then α=2; under R_2 its characteristic function is Gaussian. More generally R^k(X) converges in distribution to the centered Gaussian of variance v when EX=0 and EX²=v<∞.

## Proof

A fixed law has v=2·2^{−2/α}v, so nonzero finite v forces α=2. In that case the characteristic-function equation implies φ(t)=[φ(t/√n)]^n for dyadic n. Finite variance and centering give φ(s)=1−vs²/2+o(s²). Inserting s=t/√n and letting n grow yields exp(−vt²/2), proving the fixed-law assertion. The same expansion applied to the normalized sum of n independent copies proves convergence of characteristic functions, and hence convergence in distribution, for dyadic n and for the usual all-n central limit theorem. If higher cumulants exist, the rth cumulant scales as n^{1−r/2}; this records the disappearance of finite higher-order cumulants without asserting a uniform density approximation or a rate from only two moments.

## Worked example: setup

Let U_j be independent uniform variables on (0,1), κ_k=∏_{j=1}^kU_j and Y_j=−log U_j. Also compare a standard Cauchy sample mean.

## Worked example: calculation

Each Y_j has exponential density e^{−y} for y>0, mean and variance one. Therefore k^{−1}logκ_k→−1 almost surely and (logκ_k+k)/√k converges to N(0,1). The transform Eκ_k^{−βγ}=(1−βγ)^{−k} is finite precisely for βγ<1. For a Cauchy variable, φ(t)=e^{−|t|}; averaging n copies yields [φ(t/n)]^n=e^{−|t|}, so the mean never concentrates and P(|X̄_n|≤1)=1/2 for every n. For a Cantor coupling whose omitted tail is at most 3^{−n}, every L-Lipschitz test has expectation error at most L3^{−n}.

## Worked example: meaning

Different mechanisms supply different error statements: a weak Gaussian limit, exact Cauchy stability, a transform convergence threshold, or a deterministic transport bound. Choosing among them begins by stating which observable must be approximated and in which metric. A visual similarity between histograms does not substitute for that choice.

## Limits

Finite variance supplies no universal finite-sample normal-approximation rate. A third absolute moment enables further theorems, but is an additional hypothesis. Infinite covariance trace obstructs an ℓ² Gaussian even though each finite coordinate vector is valid. The product fixture is an iid uniform construction; dependence, atoms at zero or heavy logarithmic tails change its law. Its exact transform threshold is unrelated to an empirical phase transition unless a model is supplied.

## Open questions

For a specified non-Gaussian input with finite third moment, derive an explicit approximation error for the chosen normalized sum.

For a covariance sequence q_n, bound the mean-square tail and the error of a supplied Lipschitz functional.
