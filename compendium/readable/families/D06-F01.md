## Central question

Which conclusions follow from a declared metric, and which survive a change of scale or representation?

## Synopsis

Geometry begins with enough data to measure. A coordinate chart does not select a Riemannian metric, a positive symmetric weight table need not satisfy the triangle inequality, and a Jacobian alone does not determine singular values without input and output inner products. These distinctions let the metric material in the archive be organized by constructive outputs: path length, geodesics, curvature, volume growth and transport distance. The archive’s hyperbolic examples are particularly coherent because the metric is explicit and each claimed phenomenon can be computed from it.

On the upper half-plane with ds²=(dx²+dy²)/y², the connection, vertical unit-speed geodesics and curvature −1 agree with the disk model under the Cayley map. Constant metric rescaling changes distances, volumes, curvature and Laplace spectra in related but distinct ways. This blocks a common confusion between a geometric normalization and a dimensionless threshold. Hyperbolic volume growth gives another exact boundary: local Euclidean behavior does not imply a global doubling bound. Discrete Ollivier curvature introduces still another metric construction, comparing probability distributions by optimal transport. Its value depends on the measure convention as well as the graph, and it cannot certify unrelated computational hardness. Prefix ultrametrics supply a complementary geometry: distance is controlled by the first differing digit, while a many-to-one real coding map can erase prefix information through carries. The best synthesis therefore treats metrics as inputs whose consequences can be tested, rather than as labels attached to algebraic expressions.

## Definitions

A Riemannian metric g assigns positive inner products to tangent spaces; its path distance is the infimum of lengths of curves joining two points.

For a constant c>0, scaling g to c²g multiplies lengths by c, n-dimensional volume by cⁿ, sectional curvature by c⁻² and the scalar Laplacian by c⁻².

For graph measures m_x and m_y, Ollivier curvature on an edge is κ(x,y)=1−W₁(m_x,m_y)/d(x,y), with the measure convention declared.

On b-ary sequences, d(x,y)=a^{N(x,y)} for 0<a<1 and first-difference index N gives an ultrametric, since common prefixes concatenate by a minimum rule.

## Theorem

The metric g=(dx²+dy²)/y² has Gaussian curvature −1 and vertical unit-speed geodesics y=y₀eᵗ. Hyperbolic three-space has ball volume V₃(R)=π(sinh 2R−2R), and consequently V₃(2R)/V₃(R) is unbounded. For nonlazy uniform-neighbor measures, K₂ has edge curvature zero and K₃ has curvature 1/2.

## Proof

For the conformal plane metric e^{2u}(dx²+dy²), K=−e^{−2u}Δu. Here u=−log y, so Δu=1/y² and K=−1. A vertical path y=y₀eᵗ has speed |y′|/y=1 and satisfies the geodesic equation from Γʸ_yy=−1/y. In H³ the sphere area is 4πsinh²r. Integrating from zero to R gives π(sinh2R−2R). Its exponential leading term is (π/2)e^{2R}, so doubling the radius produces an unbounded ratio. For K₂, each nonlazy neighbor distribution is a point mass at the opposite endpoint, so W₁=1 and κ=0. For K₃, the neighbor measures share mass 1/2 at the third vertex; only the other mass 1/2 must move distance one, giving W₁=1/2. A transport plan attains that cost, and the mass discrepancy at one endpoint gives the matching lower bound.

## Worked example: setup

Fix the nonlazy neighbor convention on K₂ and K₃, and compare a constant rescaling g↦9g in the hyperbolic metric.

## Worked example: calculation

The exact transport costs are 1 and 1/2, yielding curvatures 0 and 1/2. Under 9g, the old unit vertical geodesic has speed three in the same parameter, and reparametrization by t/3 restores unit speed. Gaussian curvature becomes −1/9. A Laplace eigenvalue or quadratic-form spectral threshold scales by 1/9 as well, whereas a dimensionless ratio formed with the squared length scale can remain unchanged.

## Worked example: meaning

A metric-dependent value is meaningful when its convention is part of the result. Topological equivalence, geometric equivalence and equality of a few scalar observables remain different comparison problems.

## Limits

The exact graph checks cover two finite graphs, not a general optimal-transport solver. The hyperbolic formula assumes curvature −1 and the stated dimension. A positive weight matrix can fail metric axioms, and no unprovided metric is inferred from a historical coordinate description.

## Open questions

For a proposed metric deformation, prove positivity and the triangle property, then identify which quantities are invariant under the allowed coordinate changes.

For a claimed curvature-based certificate, state the measure convention and prove an explicit theorem linking that curvature quantity to the target property.
