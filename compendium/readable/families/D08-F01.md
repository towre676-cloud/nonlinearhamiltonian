## Central question

Which quantities are transported, conserved or dissipated by the stated evolution law and boundary conditions?

## Synopsis

Heat, nonlinear waves, reaction–diffusion and gradient flows recur across the four years. The useful common language is an evolution equation accompanied by a balance law. A conserved mass and a decreasing energy are different statements: a reaction term can destroy the first, and changing the metric of a gradient flow changes the second. Spatial boundary conditions determine whether integration by parts closes the calculation. The simplest periodic heat equation supplies a complete constructive model with a solution formula, a conserved mean and a quantitative decay estimate for every nonconstant Fourier mode. The nonlinear wave and Cahn–Hilliard members then show how the same workflow changes with the equation. An explicit profile must be substituted into that equation, rather than recognized by its shape.

## Definitions

On the circle x∈[0,2π], let u_t=κu_xx with κ>0 and periodic boundary conditions. Its spatial mean is ū=(2π)^(−1)∫u dx.

The fluctuation energy is E(t)=½∫|u−ū|²dx. For sufficiently regular u, integration by parts has no boundary contribution.

A gradient flow is defined by both an energy functional and a metric or mobility operator; L² and H^(−1) gradient flows need not conserve the same quantities.

## Theorem

Periodic heat flow conserves the mean and satisfies E′=−κ∫|u_x|²dx≤−2κE. Consequently ||u(t)−ū||₂≤e^(−κt)||u(0)−ū||₂. For a Fourier mode cos(nx), the decay rate is exactly κn².

## Proof

Integrating the PDE over one period yields d∫u/dt=κ[u_x]₀^(2π)=0, so the mean is constant. Differentiating E and integrating by parts gives E′=κ∫(u−ū)u_xx dx=−κ∫u_x²dx for a real solution; the complex case uses the real part. Expand the mean-zero fluctuation in Fourier modes. Parseval gives ∫|u_x|²=2πΣ_{n≠0}n²|û_n|²≥2πΣ_{n≠0}|û_n|²=2E. This Poincaré inequality yields E′≤−2κE; multiplying by e^(2κt) and integrating proves the stated norm estimate. Each Fourier coefficient solves û_n′=−κn²û_n, giving the exact mode formula and a spectral construction of the solution. For rough L² data, approximate by finite Fourier sums, apply the same inequality, and pass to the L² limit. This argument also exhibits its boundary dependence. For Cahn–Hilliard, u_t=Δμ with μ=δE/δu, the analogous calculation gives E′=−∫|∇μ|² and a conserved mean. For the L² flow u_t=−μ it instead gives E′=−∫μ², usually with no mass conservation. Naming an energy without specifying the mobility would miss this distinction.

## Worked example: setup

Let κ=1/2 and u(x,0)=3+2cos x−cos2x.

## Worked example: calculation

The exact solution is u(x,t)=3+2e^(−t/2)cos x−e^(−2t)cos2x. Its mean is three and E(t)=π[2e^(−t)+(1/2)e^(−4t)]. Direct differentiation gives E′=−π[2e^(−t)+2e^(−4t)], matching −κ∫|u_x|². The n=1 term makes the asymptotic norm decay rate exactly 1/2.

## Worked example: meaning

The balance law is an independent diagnostic for simulation. A purported time step or additional coupling that violates the claimed mean or energy behavior requires a new model and a new proof.

## Limits

The proved norm estimate uses a periodic fixed geometry and constant positive diffusivity. Moving domains, weak solutions, nonlinear advection and boundaries require their own regularity or flux arguments. The ignition integral is proved separately from its conditional front-speed law.
