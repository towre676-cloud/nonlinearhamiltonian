## Central question

How should a parameter-dependent local basis be chosen so logarithms, resonances and continuation data remain meaningful?

## Synopsis

Frobenius and Picard–Fuchs questions recur in scalar connections, elliptic periods, gauge changes and Hill equations. A differential equation is not determined by an evocative period name: its coefficients, singularities, normalization and initial or cycle data must be supplied. Once the operator is known, coefficient recurrence and a Wronskian provide concrete local information. When exponents collide, a raw basis can appear singular even if the solution space varies regularly. The divided-difference model below makes the logarithm appear explicitly and proves which pole is removable. It also exhibits nontrivial unipotent monodromy with eigenvalues equal to one, explaining why those eigenvalues do not imply trivial continuation. General Fuchsian systems require additional resonance compatibility conditions; the model is a constructive baseline rather than a replacement for the missing operator.

## Definitions

For t>0 let θ=t d/dt and L_ε=θ(θ+ε). Its indicial exponents are 0 and −ε.

For ε≠0 define g_ε(t)=(t^(−ε)−1)/ε. A branch of log t must be fixed for complex continuation.

A fundamental pair of scalar solutions has nonzero Wronskian. Monodromy records how a chosen basis changes after analytic continuation around a singular point.

## Theorem

g_ε extends analytically in ε to g₀=−log t. The pair (1,g_ε) remains independent with Wronskian −t^(−ε−1). Writing g_ε=Σ_{r≥0}ε^r g_r yields g_r=(−1)^(r+1)(log t)^(r+1)/(r+1)!, θ²g₀=0 and θ²g_r=−θg_(r−1) for r≥1.

## Proof

Choose a simply connected punctured domain and one analytic logarithm. Then t^(−ε)=exp(−ε log t) is entire in ε for each fixed t. Its exponential Taylor expansion shows that the numerator vanishes to first order at ε=0, so division removes the apparent pole and gives the stated coefficients. Both 1 and t^(−ε) solve L_εf=0, hence so does their divided difference. Direct differentiation gives g_ε′=−t^(−ε−1), never zero on the chosen domain; the Wronskian therefore has a finite nonzero limit −1/t. This proves independence even when the original pair (1,t^(−ε)) coalesces. Substituting the ε-series into (θ²+εθ)g=0 and equating coefficients gives the triangular hierarchy. It can also be checked directly using θ(log t)^k=k(log t)^(k−1). After a positive loop around zero, log t gains 2πi, so the limiting second solution −log t gains −2πi times the first. In the basis (1,−log t), continuation is represented by a nonidentity unipotent matrix, although both of its eigenvalues are one. The construction distinguishes a removable basis pole from genuine singular boundary data. For an actual Picard–Fuchs operator, its Frobenius recurrence and resonance equations must still be derived from that operator, rather than imported from this Euler example.

## Worked example: setup

Take t=e² and ε=1/10, and compare the exact divided difference with its first three ε terms.

## Worked example: calculation

g_(1/10)(e²)=10(e^(−1/5)−1)≈−1.812692469. The first three terms give −2+1/5−1/75=−136/75≈−1.813333333. Taylor’s theorem for e^(−1/5) bounds the absolute error after those three terms by 10(1/5)^4/4!=1/1500. The actual error is approximately 0.000640864 and lies within that bound.

## Worked example: meaning

The regularized basis, its differential equation, its Wronskian and its continuation law can all be inspected. A symbolic basis with an explicit 1/ε should not be labeled a genuinely singular solution before this test.
