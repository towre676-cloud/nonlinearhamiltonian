## Central question

Which features belong to the space, which to its cell structure, and which survive a change of coefficients?

## Synopsis

The topological material repeatedly becomes more informative after the underlying space is specified. A triangle can mean three vertices, its three-edge boundary, or a filled two-simplex. These objects have different chain groups and therefore different homology. A graph drawn on a torus is still a one-dimensional complex until its faces are attached; its cycle rank need not equal the surface’s first Betti number. Polyhedral spheres have the same homology while retaining different symmetry groups. These examples teach how to ask a sharper classification question: first choose the equivalence relation, then ask which invariant can distinguish its classes.

Integer coefficients add a second layer. Rational homology records free rank and erases torsion, so it cannot distinguish every space or quotient. A Smith normal form supplies an exact arithmetic certificate for the torsion, provided row and column operations are unimodular and the relevant quotient is actually the homology group. Reduced homology removes the connected-component contribution in degree zero; intersection homology instead restricts which chains may meet singular strata. The two operations address different questions. The cone on a torus is contractible as an ordinary space, yet lower-middle allowable chains retain two one-dimensional classes because their potential cone fillings meet the forbidden apex. A refinement is useful precisely because its allowed chains and intended invariance are explicit, rather than because the word cohomology was attached to a construction.

## Definitions

For an oriented finite simplicial complex, ∂[v₀,…,v_k]=Σ(−1)^j[v₀,…,v̂_j,…,v_k]. Its cancellation identity is ∂²=0.

A finite connected graph with V vertices and E edges has free H₁ of rank E−V+1. Attaching faces adds ∂₂ and can kill cycles.

For an integer matrix M with Smith form diag(d₁,…,d_r,0), the cokernel is ⊕Z/d_i⊕Z^{m−r}; units contribute trivial factors.

Intersection homology fixes a stratification and a perversity; allowable chain dimensions at a singular stratum determine which fillings count.

## Theorem

The triangle boundary has Betti vector (1,1), while the filled triangle has (1,0,0). A cellular model of RP² with one cell in dimensions zero, one and two has ∂₁=0 and ∂₂=2, giving H₁(RP²;Z)=Z/2 and H₂(RP²;Z)=0.

## Proof

For the triangle use edges 01,12,02 and boundary columns (−1,1,0), (0,−1,1), (−1,0,1). The matrix has rank two and its kernel is generated by (1,1,−1). With no face this is a nonzero H₁ class. Adding the oriented face 012 gives precisely that vector as ∂₂, so the quotient is zero. In the projective plane, the two-cell attaches to the one-skeleton circle by a degree-two map, hence its cellular boundary is multiplication by two. Its kernel on Z is zero and its cokernel is Z/2. Tensoring the cellular complex with Q makes multiplication by two invertible and kills H₁. Tensoring with F₂ makes that differential zero and produces classes in degrees one and two. This change illustrates why coefficients belong in the question. For the cone on T², allowable one-cycles and their bounding two-chains cannot reach a codimension-three apex at lower-middle perversity. They therefore see the punctured cone, which retracts onto T²; the two degree-one classes survive. A three-chain may reach the apex and fills the link’s two-cycle.

## Worked example: calculation

Exact rational matrix ranks yield (β₀,β₁,β₂)=(1,1,0) and (1,0,0) for the two triangle models. In the projective-plane model the Smith entry is 2, so integral first homology has a generator a with 2a=0. The rational Betti vector is (1,0,0), but the mod-two Betti vector is (1,1,1). Euler characteristics remain consistent: 1−1+1=1 for the projective-plane cell counts and for its mod-two Betti numbers.

## Worked example: meaning

A full answer records space, boundary convention, coefficient ring and the invariant’s scope. Equal Betti numbers are useful compression, not a complete geometric classification.

## Limits

The RP² calculation is cellular; a general integer homology computation must restrict ∂₂ to an integral basis of ker∂₁, rather than take coker∂₂ in the wrong ambient group. The intersection-homology cone formula depends on perversity and stratification. No complete homotopy classification is asserted.
