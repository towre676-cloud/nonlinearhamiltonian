## Central question

When can compatible local data be replaced by one global object, and how can the obstruction be calculated exactly?

## Synopsis

Gluing is a mapping problem with two distinct outcomes. A sheaf axiom says a compatible family glues uniquely; it does not say every arbitrary family is compatible or that higher cohomology vanishes. The circle examples make this distinction concrete. Two arcs overlap in two components, allowing two discrepancies; local constants can change both discrepancies only by the same amount. Their difference is therefore a one-dimensional global obstruction. This is an exact finite complex with a transparent quotient, not a violation of sheaf gluing.

The same mechanism extends to graph potentials, cellular sheaves and discrete Hodge theory. Edge values are globally integrable exactly when their cycle sums vanish. A spanning tree constructs the potential, while each remaining edge checks one cycle constraint. With matrix or scalar transport, the coefficient system changes the equation itself; the ordinary cycle of the underlying graph need not yield a nonzero sheaf class. A rank-one local system on a circle has cohomology controlled by its total monodromy. Its harmonic representative is then a metric-dependent choice inside a metric-independent quotient. This is especially relevant to source claims based on residuals: a vector with zero Hodge Laplacian residual may be a nonzero harmonic obstruction. Certifying H¹=0 requires a rank or coercivity statement on the entire relevant space, not one successful vector. Transfers between local models similarly need a chain equivalence before they can transport obstruction classes bijectively.

## Definitions

For a connected oriented graph, D:C⁰→C¹ sends a potential f to edge differences f(head)−f(tail). Gluable edge data are imD; H¹=C¹/imD when no two-cells are present.

For the constant real sheaf on a two-arc circle cover with disconnected overlap, the Čech map is R²→R², (a,b)↦(b−a,b−a).

A rank-one local system on an m-cycle has nonzero transports r_j and differential (δs)_j=s_{j+1}−r_js_j. Its loop multiplier is h=∏r_j.

## Theorem

On a connected graph, edge data are a potential difference exactly when every oriented cycle period vanishes; the potential is unique up to a constant. For the rank-one circle system, H⁰ and H¹ each have dimension one if h=1 and both vanish if h≠1.

## Proof

Necessity of zero periods follows by telescoping potential differences around a closed walk. For sufficiency choose a root and define f(v) by summing edge data along a tree path to v. A zero period on the closed walk formed with any other path proves path independence. Every edge then has the required difference; two solutions differ by a function with zero difference on every edge, hence a constant. For the local system, a global section satisfies s_{j+1}=r_js_j. Once s₀ is chosen all others follow, and closure requires (h−1)s₀=0. Thus the kernel is one-dimensional when h=1 and zero otherwise. The differential is square, so rank-nullity gives the same cokernel dimension. Rescaling the basis at vertices changes individual transports but telescoping shows their product is unchanged. In a higher-dimensional cellular sheaf, face-composition identities must additionally make δ²=0; arbitrary transport matrices alone do not guarantee a complex.

## Worked example: setup

Use a three-cycle over Q. Compare transports r=(2,3,1/6) and r=(2,3,1), with δ rows expressing the three stated equations.

## Worked example: calculation

The first product is one and (1,2,6) spans the kernel. The differential has rank two and a one-dimensional cokernel. The second product is six, forcing s₀=6s₀ and hence all section coordinates zero; the differential is invertible over Q. For additive triangle data (1,2,−3), total period is zero and f=(0,1,3) glues them. Replacing the last value by −2 gives period one and no potential. These exact checks distinguish coefficient holonomy from the graph’s ordinary cycle rank.

## Worked example: meaning

The correct interactive output is a potential or an obstruction certificate, together with the coefficient system and a gauge convention. A residual can supplement this certificate but cannot replace its quotient meaning.

## Limits

The examples concern finite graph complexes and a declared rank-one coefficient system. General sheaf cohomology needs a valid acyclic cover or an appropriate resolution. A vanishing residual for one vector cannot certify vanishing of an entire cohomology group.

## Open questions
