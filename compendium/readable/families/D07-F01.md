## Central question

When do coefficients preserve information, and how does the chosen measure control a reconstruction error?

## Synopsis

The recurring analytical object is a map from functions or vectors to coefficients. Fourier, Haar, wavelet, shearlet and character systems make different geometries visible, but every useful reconstruction statement has an ambient space, a normalization and a completeness hypothesis. The early Bessel–Parseval and Hardy–BMO questions already distinguish coefficient energy from the energy of the original object. Later sampling, weighted atoms and coherence questions add perturbations and observation loss. These are constructive ingredients for an analysis workflow: first identify which subspace the coefficients see, then calculate its norm identity or frame bounds, and finally account for noise and discarded directions. A finite example is valuable because every coefficient and residual can be inspected exactly. It does not automatically establish a continuous transform theorem or a global sampling theorem.

## Definitions

In a real or complex Hilbert space H, an orthonormal family (e_j) satisfies ⟨e_i,e_j⟩=δ_ij. Its finite projection is P_m x=Σ_{j≤m}⟨x,e_j⟩e_j.

A frame satisfies A||x||²≤Σ_j|⟨x,f_j⟩|²≤B||x||² with 0<A≤B<∞. A vanishing lower bound exposes invisible directions.

An atom supported in a set B cancels in the pairing measure μ when ∫_B a dμ=0; using cancellation in a different measure changes the argument.

## Theorem

For a finite orthonormal family, ||x||²=Σ|⟨x,e_j⟩|²+||x−P_mx||². For a finite analysis matrix T with lower frame bound A, any two candidates obey ||x−y||≤A^(−1/2)||Tx−Ty||. If each is consistent with observations to error ε, their distance is at most 2ε/√A.

## Proof

The projection residual r=x−P_mx is orthogonal to every e_j, because taking its inner product with e_k subtracts exactly the kth coefficient. Thus P_mx and r are orthogonal. Expanding ||P_mx+r||² removes the cross term, and orthonormality evaluates the projection norm as the sum of coefficient squares. This proves the identity and Bessel inequality without a completeness assumption. For a countable family, monotone convergence of the nonnegative partial sums gives the inequality; equality for every x additionally requires dense closed span. For the frame assertion apply its lower inequality to x−y and take square roots. If ||Tx−b||≤ε and ||Ty−b||≤ε, the triangle inequality bounds ||T(x−y)|| by 2ε. The conclusion is about measurement residual in the declared norm: a good residual has no such implication if A=0. Cancellation follows a parallel mechanism. Writing b_B for the μ-average, ∫ab dμ=∫a(b−b_B)dμ, so an atom bounded by 1/μ(B) pairs at most with the mean oscillation of b. This elementary estimate is the finite or single-atom part of Hardy–BMO duality; the complete representation theorem has additional content.

## Worked example: setup

Use the orthonormal Haar basis of R⁴: h₀=(1,1,1,1)/2, h₁=(1,1,−1,−1)/2, h₂=(1,−1,0,0)/√2 and h₃=(0,0,1,−1)/√2, applied to x=(1,2,3,4).

## Worked example: calculation

The coefficients are 5, −2, −1/√2 and −1/√2. Their squared sum is 25+4+1/2+1/2=30=1²+2²+3²+4². Keeping only h₀,h₁ gives the block-average reconstruction (3/2,3/2,7/2,7/2); its residual has squared norm one, exactly the energy of the omitted detail coefficients.

## Worked example: meaning

This calculation makes approximation loss measurable before any interpretation of the four entries. A prime-gap vector, image row or sensor trace uses the same finite identity, while the statistical meaning of its coefficients requires separate assumptions.

## Limits

Completeness, a positive lower frame bound and a matched measure cannot be inferred from a visually plausible transform. FFT differentiation differentiates the trigonometric interpolant; aliasing and unknown high frequencies remain outside that certificate.
