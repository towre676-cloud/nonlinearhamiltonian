## Central question

Which curve geometry is fixed by curvature, and when does a variational principle actually select a shape?

## Synopsis

Curves provide a manageable laboratory for separating geometry, coordinates and dynamics. An affine transformation composes by the semidirect rule, while a time-dependent coordinate frame adds velocity and acceleration terms. A Frenet frame follows a curve’s tangent, normal and binormal; its rotation can be written using a Darboux angular-velocity vector. Neither operation by itself is a new physical law. The archive’s most useful curve examples become explicit once the parameter, regularity and objective are fixed: winding requires a nonvanishing loop, spirograph closure requires commensurate frequencies, and a clothoid minimizes one declared curvature-rate energy with endpoint curvatures.

The variational distinction is especially important. Minimizing the squared derivative of curvature under fixed endpoint curvatures selects linear curvature, but prescribing endpoint positions and tangent directions adds integral constraints and changes the admissible problem. A clothoid is therefore an exact solution of a clearly stated functional, not a universal optimal connector. Extrinsic surface geometry makes a parallel point about normalization: if mean curvature is the average of the principal curvatures, a round sphere has Willmore energy 4π at every radius; using their sum changes the scale by four. Surface immersion, embedding and eversion are also different questions. The curve and surface material consequently contributes a practical discipline: identify the geometric object, pick invariant quantities, state the boundary data and only then optimize or classify.

## Definitions

For a unit-speed regular space curve with nonzero curvature, the Frenet equations are T′=κN, N′=−κT+τB and B′=−τN. Their Darboux vector is Ω=τT+κB.

For a planar unit-speed curve, θ′=κ and γ′=(cosθ,sinθ). A curvature function plus an initial position and tangent determines the curve by integration.

Fix L>0 and endpoint curvatures κ(0)=κ₀, κ(L)=κ₁. The curvature-rate objective is J[κ]=(1/2)∫₀ᴸ(κ′)²ds on absolutely continuous κ with square-integrable derivative.

With averaged mean curvature H=(κ₁+κ₂)/2, the Willmore functional is ∫H²dA. Its normalization must be stated before numerical comparison.

## Theorem

The unique minimizer of the stated curvature-rate problem is κ(s)=κ₀+(κ₁−κ₀)s/L, with J_min=(κ₁−κ₀)²/(2L). The associated tangent angle is θ₀+κ₀s+(κ₁−κ₀)s²/(2L). A round sphere has averaged-mean-curvature Willmore energy 4π independently of radius.

## Proof

Write κ=κ_lin+η with η(0)=η(L)=0. Expanding J gives J[κ]=J[κ_lin]+(κ₁−κ₀)∫η′/L+(1/2)∫(η′)². The cross term vanishes because the endpoint difference of η is zero. The last term is nonnegative, with equality only when η is constant, hence zero. This proves existence and uniqueness without assuming an unproved Euler–Lagrange conclusion. Integrating θ′=κ gives the quadratic phase, and integrating the unit tangent yields Fresnel-type coordinates. For a sphere of radius R, both principal curvatures have magnitude 1/R, so H²=1/R² and area=4πR², giving 4π. The Frenet–Darboux identity follows directly from the oriented orthonormal cross-product relations: Ω×T=κN, Ω×N=−κT+τB and Ω×B=−τN. It describes the same frame rotation rather than an additional dynamical postulate.

## Worked example: setup

Set L=2, κ₀=0, κ₁=2 and θ₀=0. Compare linear curvature with κ_a(s)=s+a s(2−s).

## Worked example: calculation

The minimizer is κ=s with θ=s²/2 and J=1. Since κ_a′=1+2a−2as, direct integration gives J[κ_a]=1+4a²/3, strictly larger for a≠0. For a moving affine frame y=M(t)x+b(t), differentiation gives ẏ=Mẋ+Ṁx+ḃ and ÿ=Mẍ+2Ṁẋ+M̈x+b̈; the extra terms are coordinate effects determined by the frame. For a two-frequency spirograph with both coefficients nonzero, rational frequency ratio gives a common period, while irrational ratio prevents one by uniqueness of finite exponential sums.

## Limits

Frenet coordinates fail where curvature vanishes and need another frame. The clothoid theorem does not impose endpoint positions or fixed total turning. Sphere eversion uses immersions and can pass through self-intersections; it is not an isotopy of embedded spheres.

## Open questions

For a connector with fixed endpoint positions and tangents, formulate the additional integral constraints and derive the constrained Euler–Lagrange system.

For a proposed curve classification, determine whether the chosen invariants separate curves up to the exact rigid-motion, reparametrization or homotopy relation intended.
