## Central question

When does a recurrence describe every integer solution, and how do finite congruence filters change an infinite population?

## Synopsis

A norm-preserving recurrence generates solutions immediately; proving that it generates all of them is the substantive step. The D=2 Pell equation has an elementary descent to the identity, while the negative equation x²−2y²=−7 requires two distinct canonical seeds. A compact reduction interval makes the seed count finite and proves uniqueness. This exposes an important difference between a finite list, an infinite parametrized family, and a complete classification. Once completeness is established, modular arithmetic is useful again: the unit recurrence is invertible modulo every q, so its state orbit is genuinely periodic. Counting accepted index classes gives an exact density within the orbit and a logarithmic coefficient in a height-bounded population. The same quadratic-unit mechanism explains Fibonacci matrix eigenvalues, but geometric length normalizations must still be declared separately.

## Definitions

In Z[√2], conjugation sends x+y√2 to x−y√2 and the norm is x²−2y². The norm-one unit ε=3+2√2 has inverse 3−2√2.

Multiplication by ε sends (x,y) to (3x+4y,2x+3y), an integral matrix of determinant one.

A canonical seed is a norm solution reduced to a specified half-open interval under multiplication by ε. A modular filter is a predicate on the full residue state, not solely on a visually selected coordinate.

## Theorem

Every solution x,y≥0 of x²−2y²=1 is ε^j for a unique j≥0. Every solution of x²−2y²=−7 with y>0 belongs uniquely to (1+2√2)ε^j or (5+4√2)ε^j, j∈Z. For the positive Pell orbit, a modulo-q predicate accepting w positions in its period P yields a population (w/P)log N/log ε+O(1) under y≤N.

## Proof

For norm one and y>0, y≥2 and √2<x/y≤3/2. The inverse step gives x′=3x−4y>0 and 0≤y′=3y−2x<y; iteration reaches (1,0), proving coverage. For norm −7, α=x+y√2 is positive whenever y>0. Multiply by a unique power of ε to put β in [√7,ε√7). Since β̄=−7/β, its coordinates satisfy x_β=(β−7/β)/2≥0 and y_β=(β+7/β)/(2√2), which lies below 3√(7/2)<6. Thus y_β∈{2,3,4,5}; the required squares 2y_β²−7 are 1,11,25,43. Exactly two seeds remain. The interval is half-open, so seed and exponent are unique. For the positive orbit, conjugation gives y_j=(ε^j−ε^(−j))/(2√2), hence j≤log N/log ε+O(1). The determinant-one recurrence permutes a finite modular state set, so its orbit has no transient tail. A periodic acceptance pattern with w of P positions contributes w/P of the indices, up to a bounded endpoint error.

## Worked example: setup

Generate the two negative-norm seeds, then apply an even-index filter to the positive-norm orbit.

## Worked example: calculation

The first seed (1,2) maps to (11,8), and 11²−2·8²=−7. The second (5,4) maps to (31,22), also of norm −7. The inverse sends (11,8) back to (1,2). The positive norm-one sequence begins (3,2),(17,12),(99,70),(577,408). Modulo 3 its full states (x,y) are (1,0),(0,2),(2,0),(0,1), followed by (1,0); the period is four. The condition y≡0 mod 3 accepts precisely the even indices, so the filtered count has coefficient 1/(2log ε).

## Worked example: meaning

Completeness rests on descent and seed reduction; finite modular enumeration then safely determines the accepted fraction of that already complete family.

## Limits

The lab covers these D=2 equations. A different discriminant or norm can have more seeds, a different unit group and nontrivial sign conventions. Floating-point logarithms may display growth but are not used to certify integer membership. No unsupplied global shift-uniqueness theorem follows from the population calculation.

## Open questions

For each additional quadratic core, can all seeds be bounded and enumerated with a proof of descent coverage before attaching any congruence filter?

Can an exact interval method decide a height cutoff without rounding error when ε^j lies close to the chosen bound?
