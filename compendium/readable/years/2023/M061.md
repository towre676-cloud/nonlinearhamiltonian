## Revised question retained in the annual source

Under which moment assumptions is the covariance matrix defined, positive semidefinite and usable for a quadratic objective?

## Status

Classical result developed and worked

## Summary

Covariance optimization is legitimate only after existence of the required moments is established. Once variables are square integrable, covariance becomes a positive semidefinite Gram matrix in an L² space, and the familiar bounds follow from one short argument.

## Definitions

For square-integrable X and Y, Cov(X,Y)=E[(X−EX)overline(Y−EY)]. For a real random vector Z, its covariance matrix Σ has entries Cov(Z_i,Z_j). Positive semidefinite means wᵀΣw≥0 for every w.

## Result name

Covariance as a Gram matrix and its equality condition

## Result statement

|Cov(X,Y)|²≤Var(X)Var(Y). For a finite square-integrable real vector, Var(wᵀZ)=wᵀΣw. If both variances are positive, equality in the scalar bound occurs precisely when the centered variables are proportional almost surely.

## Proof

Centered variables are elements of the Hilbert space L². Cauchy–Schwarz gives the bound. Alternatively, for real variables, nonnegativity of E[(X−EX−t(Y−EY))²] for every real t forces the discriminant of this quadratic in t to be nonpositive. Expanding the centered weighted sum proves the matrix identity and hence positive semidefiniteness. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz identifies linear dependence in L², where two representatives differing only on a null set are equivalent.

## Worked example: setup

Let X be equally likely to be −1 or 1, and let Y=2X.

## Worked example: calculation

Both means vanish. Variances are 1 and 4, covariance is 2, and Σ=[[1,2],[2,4]] has determinant zero. The combination 2X−Y vanishes almost surely, so its variance is zero. This explains the singularity structurally rather than treating it as a numerical failure.

## Worked example: conclusion

A singular covariance matrix records an exact L² dependency. It can be essential information for a constrained quadratic calculation.

## Limits

The Cauchy example M057 is outside these hypotheses; finite-sample covariance numbers do not create a population covariance that fails to exist. For complex variables, conjugation must be retained. Estimated matrices may require a separate analysis of sampling error.
