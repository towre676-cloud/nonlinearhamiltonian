## Revised question retained in the annual source

What does the Gudermannian simplify, and what Jacobian and endpoint singularities does the simplification introduce?

## Status

An exact coordinate transformation and endpoint asymptotic

## Summary

The Gudermannian is a genuine bridge between hyperbolic and circular expressions. Its advantage is algebraic simplification on a bounded angular interval; its cost is a singular inverse-coordinate derivative near that interval’s endpoints.

## Definitions

Set θ=gd(x)=arctan(sinh x), mapping R bijectively to (−π/2,π/2).

Then sinθ=tanh x, cosθ=sech x and θ′=cosθ.

The inverse is x=asinh(tanθ)=artanh(sinθ), with dx/dθ=secθ.

## Result name

Transforming derivatives and integrals consistently

## Result statement

For a smooth function F(θ), ∂_xF=cosθ F_θ and ∂_x²F=cos²θ F_θθ−sinθ cosθ F_θ. For an integrable profile u(x)=U(gd(x)), ∫_R u(x)dx=∫_(−π/2)^(π/2)U(θ)secθ dθ. The angular compression does not discard this Jacobian.

## Proof

Differentiate θ=arctan(sinh x): θ′=cosh x/(1+sinh²x)=sech x. Its range and monotonicity show it is a bijection. Since cosθ is positive in that range, the elementary identities follow by resolving a right triangle with tangent sinh x. The differential operator is ∂_x=cosθ∂_θ; applying it twice gives the stated second derivative. The ordinary change-of-variables theorem supplies dx=secθdθ. For x→+∞, π/2−gd(x)=2arctan(e^(−x))∼2e^(−x). Hence the inverse sensitivity secθ grows like 1/(π/2−θ). Bounded angular coordinates therefore do not guarantee bounded recovery error in x.

## Worked example: setup

Transform u(x)=Asech²x.

## Worked example: calculation

The angular profile is U(θ)=Acos²θ. Retaining the measure gives ∫u dx=A∫cosθdθ=2A, matching the direct calculation A[tanh x]_(−∞)^∞. If one integrated Acos²θ against dθ without the Jacobian, the answer would instead be Aπ/2 and would describe a different measure.

## Worked example: conclusion

The transformation simplifies a profile while preserving its integral only when the derivative and measure factors are carried along.

## Limits

For a moving coordinate θ=gd(κ(x−ct)), time differentiation also contributes θ_t=−cκcosθ. A stationary spatial formula cannot simply be inserted into a time-dependent PDE without this extra chain rule.
