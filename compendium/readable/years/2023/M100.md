## Revised question retained in the annual source

For which polynomial f can we calculate its b-function and verify the functional equation explicitly?

## Status

Classical result developed and example checked

## Summary

The original broad application question becomes an exact operator identity with a lower-bound argument. A certificate proves that a polynomial works; minimality proves that it is the b-function. Both steps can be completed for monomials, and a linear coordinate change supplies a nonmonomial example.

## Definitions

Work over C in the Weyl algebra generated by x and ∂ with ∂x−x∂=1. The parameter s commutes with both generators.

A Bernstein–Sato relation is P(s)f^(s+1)=b(s)f^s in the formal localization module. The global b-function is the monic polynomial of least degree satisfying such a relation.

## Result name

Exact monomial b-function

## Result statement

For m≥1, b_(x^m)(s)=∏_(j=1)^m(s+j/m), with certificate P(s)=m^(−m)∂^m.

## Proof

Differentiate x^(m(s+1)) exactly m times. The coefficient is ∏_(k=0)^(m−1)(m(s+1)−k)=m^m∏_(j=1)^m(s+j/m), proving the certificate. To prove minimality, put an arbitrary polynomial-coefficient operator in normal order Σ a_ij(s)x^i∂^j. Its action contributes the Laurent monomial x^(ms+m+i−j). The coefficient of x^(ms) can only receive terms with j=m+i. Every such falling-factorial coefficient contains all m factors from the displayed certificate. The other Laurent powers are independent over C(s), so any possible b(s) is divisible by that monic product. Thus the candidate is minimal, not merely an annihilating multiple.

## Worked example: setup

Take f=x²y³ and then the nonmonomial f=x²+y².

## Worked example: calculation

For x²y³, P=(1/108)∂ₓ²∂ᵧ³ gives b(s)=(s+1/2)(s+1/3)(s+2/3)(s+1)². The same exponent-comparison argument in both variables proves minimality. Over C set u=x+iy and v=x−iy. Then x²+y²=uv and ∂ᵤ∂ᵥ=(1/4)(∂ₓ²+∂ᵧ²), so (1/4)Δf^(s+1)=(s+1)²f^s.

## Worked example: conclusion

A polynomial automorphism preserves the defining differential-operator problem, so the nonmonomial quadratic has b(s)=(s+1)². Repeated roots carry actual multiplicity; they must not be discarded.

## Limits

These are global polynomial examples in characteristic zero. General singularities may require local rather than global b-functions, elimination in a Weyl algebra, or additional singularity theory. The derivation does not establish that every proposed physical or statistical application admits a useful b-function.

## Extension

A worthwhile next calculation is to compute a local b-function for a chosen non-normal-crossing plane singularity, retaining the operator certificate and a separate minimality argument. An unverified candidate formula should be labeled as such.
