## Revised question retained in the annual source

Which observations support a Cauchy tail, and what estimators remain meaningful if the mean and variance fail to exist?

## Status

Classical result developed and worked

## Summary

The useful revision is a complete separation between symmetry, existence of a mean, and concentration of an estimator. The Cauchy family gives an exact example in which averaging independent observations does not improve the distribution of the sample mean. This is a classical stability calculation, not a conclusion about any particular empirical dataset.

## Definitions

A Cauchy law with location μ and scale γ>0 has density γ/[π((x−μ)²+γ²)]. Its characteristic function is φ(t)=exp(iμt−γ|t|). Independence multiplies characteristic functions.

## Result name

Exact failure of sample-mean concentration

## Result statement

If X₁,…,Xₙ are independent Cauchy(μ,γ), then their arithmetic mean is again Cauchy(μ,γ), for every positive integer n. Neither E[X] nor Var(X) exists as an ordinary finite moment.

## Proof

The positive and negative parts of the centered first-moment integral each diverge logarithmically because x/(1+x²) has a logarithmic antiderivative. Symmetric truncation cancels these divergences, but that principal value is not a Lebesgue expectation. For the mean, independence gives E exp(itΣXⱼ/n)=φ(t/n)ⁿ=exp(iμt−γ|t|). Uniqueness of characteristic functions identifies the law. Thus no finite-variance central-limit estimate can be invoked. The same tail calculation gives a divergent second moment.

## Worked example: setup

Take standard Cauchy observations and ask whether the mean is within one unit of zero.

## Worked example: calculation

For every n, P(|X̄ₙ|≤1)=F(1)−F(−1)=1/2, using F(x)=1/2+arctan(x)/π. The probability remains one half even when n increases. In contrast, the population median is μ and independent sample quantiles can consistently estimate it because F is continuous and strictly increasing there.

## Worked example: conclusion

A stable distribution can make an apparently reasonable estimator permanently noisy. Quantile estimation and distribution fitting must be evaluated under their actual assumptions.

## Limits

This does not establish that observations are Cauchy, that every heavy-tailed law lacks a mean, or that all robust estimators have the same behavior. Dependence would also change the characteristic-function multiplication.

## Extension

For a specified tail exponent, determine which absolute moments exist, then compare mean, median and trimmed estimators using exact or justified asymptotic distributions.
