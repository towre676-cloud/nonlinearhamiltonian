## Revised question retained in the annual source

What does a theorem proved from named regularity axioms actually establish?

## Status

Classical or newly chosen explicit model developed with proof; historical conjectures delimited

## Summary

The recovered Leanfiles retainedaxioms and anadmitat the production-versus-dissipation step, while earlier output claimed compilation and completion. A precise logical model distinguishes a valid conditional theorem from evidence that its analytic hypotheses hold. The same distinction applies to a certifier that inserts its target matrix.

## Definitions

Suppose statements P, Q, Rstand for disorder, intermittency and transverse regeneration, and Gfor global regularity.

A theorem deriving Gfrom assumptions P, Q, Rproves(P∧Q∧R)⇒G; it does not prove the antecedent.

## Result name

Countermodel test for an alleged unconditional conclusion

## Result statement

The implication(P∧Q∧R)⇒Gcan be true even when Gis false, because the antecedent may be false. Likewise verifying eigenvalues of a hard-coded matrix certifies that matrix, not its provenance from an unavailable source operator.

## Proof

Use a Boolean model with P=false, Q=true, R=true, G=false. The conjunction is false, and material implication fromfalseis true. Thus the conditional theorem can be logically valid in a model where Gfails, proving that unconditional Gdoes not follow from that implication alone. In a proof assistant, the theorem’s dependency list records the assumed constants or axioms used; compilation checks the derivation relative to them. Replacing a missing analytic estimate by an axiom moves it into the assumptions rather than solving it. For matrix certification, the same separation is algebraic:if M₂=[[5/8,631/25000],[0,5/8]]is declared, its characteristic polynomial is(t−5/8)²and its nilpotent residual is square-zero. Those facts do not prove M₂was extracted from Catmull–Clarkdata.
