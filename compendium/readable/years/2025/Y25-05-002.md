## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: What does a curl-flux integral actually determine, and which extra hypotheses would make it independent of the chosen surface?

## Status

Developed mathematical repair or classical result; speculative source claims delimited

## Summary

The external topic label combines a wavelet with curl-flux integrals and proposed topological invariants. A precise first step is Stokes’ theorem. It explains what is surface independent, what changes with the vector field, and why an arbitrary real-valued integral is not automatically a topological invariant.

## Definitions

Let S be a compact oriented piecewise smooth surface in R³ with oriented boundary C. Let F be C¹ on a neighborhood of S.

Define Φ_F(S)=∫_S(curl F)·n dA. Boundary orientation is chosen by the usual right-hand convention. For a closed surface assume the additional smoothness needed for the divergence theorem.

## Result name

Stokes independence with fixed boundary, and a counterexample to topological invariance

## Result statement

Φ_F(S)=∮_C F·dr. Consequently two admissible surfaces with the same oriented boundary give the same integral for the same field. The value can nevertheless vary continuously under changes of F while the surface topology stays fixed.

## Proof

Stokes’ theorem is the explicit classical input identifying the surface integral with circulation. Applying it to S₁ and S₂ with the same C gives equal values, provided F is defined smoothly on both. For a closed surface bounding a region with a smooth field, divergence of curl is zero, so its flux is zero; this also follows from Stokes because the boundary is empty. To show that no nontrivial topological invariant follows merely from the integral, use F_a(x,y,z)=a(−y,x,0)/2. Its curl is (0,0,a). On the unit disk in the xy-plane the flux is aπ, which ranges over all real values as a changes. The disk’s topology has not changed. The equality with circulation still holds, so the failure is in the proposed interpretation rather than in Stokes’ theorem.

## Worked example: setup

Use the upward-oriented disk of radius two and F=(−y,x,0)/2.

## Worked example: calculation

The curl is (0,0,1), so the flux is the area 4π. Parameterize its boundary by r(t)=(2cos t,2sin t,0). Then F(r(t))=(−sin t,cos t,0) and F·r′=2, giving ∫₀^(2π)2dt=4π independently. Reversing orientation changes the sign. Scaling F by three gives 12π without changing the disk or boundary shape.

## Worked example: conclusion

The calculation gives a complete boundary certificate and identifies exactly why the adjective topological needs further structure.

## Limits

Singular fields, punctured domains and nonbounding cycles can carry additional information; the smooth-neighborhood assumptions cannot be dropped silently. No new invariant of knots or wavelets is proved here.

## Extension

Replace the arbitrary vector field by a closed differential form with a specified integral cohomology class. Then periods on homologous cycles agree, and integer normalization can give genuinely topological data. The closedness and integral-class assumptions are the required mathematical work.
