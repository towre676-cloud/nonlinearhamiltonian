## Revised question retained in the annual source

What can a rate-times-duration calculation establish, and how does a changing rate alter cumulative totals?

## Status

Developed mathematical treatment with explicit hypotheses, derivation and checked example

## Summary

An integral is the correct link between incidence flow and cumulative count. Exact examples and bounds show what a constant-rate scenario means, while separating a stock of simultaneous jobs from accumulated person-years or unique people.

## Definitions

Let r(t)≥0 be an event rate in events per year on [0,T]. The cumulative count is C=∫₀ᵀr(t)dt.

Let e(t)>0 be exposure, such as person-years or distance per unit time. A normalized incidence is r(t)/e(t), with explicitly different units.

Let J(t) count jobs occupied at a moment. ∫J(t)dt measures job-years, not unique workers.

## Result name

Rate bounds and an exact growth model

## Result statement

If r_min≤r(t)≤r_max, then Tr_min≤C≤Tr_max. If r(t)=r₀e^{gt}, then C=r₀(e^{gT}−1)/g for g≠0 and C=r₀T for g=0. A constant-rate product is an assumption, not an automatically valid historical estimate or lower bound.

## Proof

Integrating the pointwise rate inequalities gives the bounds immediately. Integrating the exponential gives the closed form, whose limit at g=0 follows from the derivative of exp at zero. The average rate is C/T; a single endpoint value does not determine it. For example, if the rate has been increasing, its present value multiplied by T is an upper scenario rather than a lower bound. Exposure introduces another distinction: a falling event-per-mile rate can coexist with a rising raw event count if total distance grows fast enough. No arithmetic conversion recovers missing historical incidence data.

## Worked example: setup

Compare two new 75-year scenarios, each ending at one million events per year.

## Worked example: calculation

A constant rate gives 75 million. A linearly rising rate from .2 million to one million has average .6 million and total 45 million. Separately, ten million jobs continuously occupied for 75 years yield 750 million job-years; with complete yearly turnover, the number of distinct workers could differ radically from the no-turnover case despite identical J(t).

## Worked example: conclusion

The calculation records the assumed trajectory and units, so its result cannot be mistaken for measured historical totals.

## Limits
