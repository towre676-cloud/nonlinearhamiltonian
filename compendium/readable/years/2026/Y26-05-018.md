## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: What is the theoretical hydrate mass implied by a magnesium concentration, and how should losses enter?

## Status

Classical result or newly stipulated finite model; historical claims explicitly delimited

## Summary

The source discussion was industrial chemistry, but its mathematical core is a stoichiometric conversion and a throughput balance. We extract only that mathematics and do not evaluate business viability, process safety or measured yield.

## Definitions

Use the stipulated molar masses Mg=24.305, Cl=35.45 and H₂O=18.015 grams per mole. One mole MgCl₂·6H₂O contains one mole magnesium.

Let c be magnesium mass per unit water volume, V a water volume and η∈[0,1] the fraction of magnesium recovered into the stated pure hydrate.

## Result name

Stoichiometric recovery is a dimensionally exact mass balance with an explicit yield parameter

## Result statement

The hydrate mass is m=ηcV M_h/M_Mg, where M_h=24.305+2(35.45)+6(18.015)=203.295. Consequently theoretical mass scales linearly with feed volume, magnesium concentration and recovery fraction; changing the hydrate water content changes the conversion factor.

## Proof

The feed contains cV mass units of magnesium. Dividing by its molar mass gives moles of magnesium; the one-to-one formula-unit relation gives the same number of hydrate moles before losses. Multiply by M_h and by η to obtain the stated equation. Units cancel as (mass/volume)(volume)(mass/mole)/(mass/mole). The formula cannot produce more magnesium in the output than entered the process because the output magnesium mass is exactly ηcV. Hydration adds mass from water and chloride, so hydrate mass exceeding magnesium mass is not a conservation violation. Conversely, the formula alone does not show that enough chloride, appropriate solution conditions or a suitable separation process is available. Those are separate process assumptions, not consequences of arithmetic.

## Worked example: setup

Stipulate c=1.3 kg Mg per cubic metre and V=10 cubic metres.

## Worked example: calculation
