## Revised question retained in the annual source

Which hypotheses distinguish solving an equation over integers, rationals, reals or complex numbers?

## Status

Developed mathematical treatment with explicit hypotheses, derivation and checked example

## Summary

A linear Diophantine equation and a quadratic illustrate how the chosen scalar domain changes both existence and structure. The domains are connected by embeddings, but their divisibility, completeness and order properties cannot be interchanged.

## Definitions

Use N={1,2,…} and N₀={0,1,2,…}; this convention makes N⊂N₀⊂Z⊂Q⊂R⊂C.

For integers a,b not both zero, gcd(a,b) is their positive greatest common divisor. A Diophantine solution requires integer unknowns.

A field allows division by every nonzero element. An ordered field has an order compatible with addition and multiplication; C cannot carry such an order.

## Result name

Solvability criteria and a domain boundary

## Result statement

ax+by=c has an integer solution exactly when gcd(a,b) divides c. The equation x²=2 has no rational solution but has two real solutions; x²=−1 has no real solution but has solutions ±i in C.

## Proof

Every integer linear combination of a,b is divisible by their gcd, proving necessity. Euclid’s algorithm yields integers u,v with au+bv=gcd(a,b); multiply by c/gcd(a,b) for sufficiency. If x²=2 with x=r/s in lowest positive terms, r²=2s² makes r even; writing r=2k makes s even as well, contradicting coprimality. Completeness of R supplies √2, for example as the supremum of positive rationals with square below two. In an ordered field every square is nonnegative; therefore i²=−1 rules out an ordered-field structure on C extending ordinary arithmetic.

## Worked example: setup

Compare 6x+9y=3 with 6x+9y=1.

## Worked example: calculation

The first has integer solution (−1,1) because −6+9=3, and all solutions are x=−1+3t,y=1−2t for t∈Z. The second has no integer solution since three does not divide one, but has rational solution (1/6,0). Thus even an elementary equation needs its coefficient and solution domains declared.

## Worked example: conclusion

Embeddings preserve arithmetic identities while new elements can change solvability and available analytic tools.

## Limits

This does not classify every equation or foundational system. Complex square-root notation is multivalued unless a branch is chosen, and completeness of R is an additional property rather than a formal consequence of being a field.

## Extension

For a polynomial or linear system, compare solutions under field extension and integral constraints, compute exact obstruction invariants, and state which analytic arguments require completeness rather than algebra alone.
