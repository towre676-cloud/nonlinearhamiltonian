## Revised question retained in the annual source

Why does eliminating a variable preserve a partition sum, and when does introducing a functional gate preserve the original model count?

## Status

Classical mathematics or a newly chosen finite model developed with proof; historical claims delimited

## Summary

The source distinguishes sparse elimination from implementation benchmarks and records a gate-projection bijection. Both can be proved directly by finite sums. Auxiliary variables are harmless only when their number of extensions is exactly controlled for every original assignment.

## Definitions

Let variables range over finite sets and let Z=Σ_x∏_j f_j(x_scope(j)) over a commutative semiring, such as nonnegative integers.

For a Boolean relation F(x,a), let m(x)=|{a:F(x,a)}| be the auxiliary fibre size.

## Result name

Exact elimination and unique-extension projection

## Result statement

Eliminating v replaces all factors involving v by g(y)=Σ_v∏_(j:v∈scope(j))f_j, leaving other factors unchanged and preserving Z. If F(x,a) encodes a predicate P(x) with exactly one auxiliary extension for each satisfying x and none otherwise, then #F=#P.

## Proof

In the finite sum defining Z, factors independent of v can be moved outside the sum over v by distributivity. The remaining sum is precisely g, proving elimination. Repeating this operation in any order preserves the same partition sum, although intermediate scopes and cost can differ greatly. For projection, partition the satisfying assignments of F by their original x-coordinate. Its count is Σ_x m(x), which equals #P exactly when m(x)=1 on P and zero off P. Merely proving existence of an auxiliary extension proves equisatisfiability, not count equality. If the fibre size is a known constant c on P, the count is c#P and a correction is possible; varying multiplicity needs weights or explicit projection. The argument also explains why duplicate-looking original coordinates must not be merged without their multiplicities.

## Worked example: setup

Introduce the gate a=x₁ XOR x₂.

## Worked example: calculation

All four binary choices of (x₁,x₂) have exactly one admissible a, so the gate relation has four satisfying triples. If a is instead unconstrained, the count is eight although both formulas project to the same four original assignments. Adding the condition a=1 gives precisely the two original inputs 01 and 10. Eliminating a by summing its gate indicator times its output indicator yields the XOR indicator on the original variables.

## Worked example: conclusion

The count-preserving gate theorem is verified on every fibre rather than inferred from satisfiability.

## Limits

The source’s benchmark solve counts and timing comparisons require raw instances and execution receipts; the algebraic theorem does not reproduce them. Real-valued signed factors can define a partition sum without defining a probability distribution.

## Extension

Generate elimination certificates as exact local tables or symbolic factors, attach a unique-extension proof to each auxiliary gate, and check the final scalar independently of the optimizer used to choose elimination order.
