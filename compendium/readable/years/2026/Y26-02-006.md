## Revised question retained in the annual source

How can a two-component expansion preserve information that ordinary floating-point accumulation loses?

## Status

Developed mathematical treatment with explicit hypotheses, derivation and checked example

## Summary

A cancellation example shows exactly why a persistent low component can matter. An error-free sum transform stores the rounded sum and its residual, but neither prefix geometry nor a count-only change guarantees a general optimization benefit.

## Definitions

Use IEEE-style radix-two round-to-nearest arithmetic with sufficient exponent range and no overflow; a floating value is interpreted as its exact represented real number.

For |a|≥|b|, FastTwoSum computes s=fl(a+b), z=fl(s−a), r=fl(b−z). Under its standard rounding assumptions, s+r=a+b exactly.

An expansion represents a real number by the unevaluated sum of high and low floating components; collapsing it prematurely loses the recovered residual.

## Result name

Exact residual and a cancellation witness

## Result statement

For binary64 a=2^53 and b=1, the rounded sum is s=2^53 and the residual is r=1. Thus subsequently subtracting 2^53 from the represented pair recovers one, while left-associated scalar arithmetic gives zero.

## Proof

The spacing immediately above 2^53 is two. The exact value 2^53+1 lies halfway between adjacent representable numbers and ties-to-even rounds to 2^53. Consequently z=fl(s−a)=0 and r=fl(1−0)=1, proving the exact decomposition for this witness directly. Subtracting a from the high component then leaves high zero and low one. More generally FastTwoSum’s ordering condition ensures the subtraction extracting the high contribution is exact, and the remaining error is representable; the full error-free-transform theorem is background under the stated rounding model. A persistent low component must be updated through subsequent operations rather than dropped at an arbitrary splice.

Evaluate a three-term sum containing severe cancellation.

## Worked example: calculation

Ordinary evaluation fl(fl(2^53+1)−2^53) returns 0, although the real result is 1. Keeping the pair (2^53,1) through cancellation returns 1. Reordering as 2^53+fl(1−2^53) can give a different path, illustrating that a declared accumulation algorithm is part of the numerical method.

## Worked example: conclusion

The example supplies a reproducible arithmetic mechanism, independent of reported training metrics.

## Limits

The exact transform depends on floating-point assumptions and does not by itself bound an entire optimizer’s accumulated error. A recorded improvement or failure in historical experiments is not independently reproduced here.

## Extension
