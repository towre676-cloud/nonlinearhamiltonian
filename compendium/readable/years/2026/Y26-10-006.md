## Revised question retained in the annual source

Can the integer solutions of x²+7=2y² be organized into complete transport orbits rather than generated only experimentally?

## Status

Classical mathematics or a newly chosen finite model developed with proof; historical claims delimited

## Summary

The recovered quadratic equation is a negative Pell equation. Multiplication by the fundamental norm-one unit gives exact transport, and reduction to a compact interval produces a finite seed proof. This supplies the global completeness argument missing from a table of recurrent solutions.

## Definitions

In Z[√2], N(x+y√2)=x²−2y². Let ε=3+2√2, with N(ε)=1 and ε⁻¹=3−2√2.

For y>0 and N(α)=−7, the real number α=x+y√2 is positive because |x|<√2y.

## Result name

Two-orbit completeness

## Result statement

Every solution with y>0 is given uniquely by α=(1+2√2)εⁿ or α=(5+4√2)εⁿ for n∈Z. Solutions with y<0 are obtained by negation; y=0 gives none.

## Proof

Multiplying by ε sends (x,y) to (3x+4y,2x+3y), an invertible integer map preserving norm. For any positive α of norm −7 there is a unique n such that β=αε^(−n) lies in [√7,ε√7). Its conjugate is −7/β, so its coordinates satisfy x_β=(β−7/β)/2≥0 and y_β=(β+7/β)/(2√2). On this interval y_β≥√(7/2) and y_β<3√(7/2)<6, hence y_β is one of 2,3,4,5. The equation x_β²=2y_β²−7 gives respectively 1,11,25,43; only y_β=2,4 yield squares. Thus β is exactly 1+2√2 or 5+4√2. Both lie in the reduction interval, and the half-open interval gives uniqueness of the exponent and seed. Conversely, multiplying either seed by any integer power of ε preserves integral coordinates, positive α and norm −7; then y=(α+7/α)/(2√2)>0. This proves both coverage and absence of duplicates.

## Worked example: setup

Generate and reverse a transported solution.

## Worked example: calculation

The first seed (1,2) maps to (11,8), since 3+8=11 and 2+6=8; indeed 121+7=2·64. The second seed (5,4) maps to (31,22). Applying ε⁻¹ sends (11,8) to (3·11−4·8,−2·11+3·8)=(1,2).

## Worked example: conclusion

Two seeds and an invertible recurrence describe the entire integer solution set, with the sign cases explicit.

## Limits

A norm-preserving recurrence by itself proves only closure. Completeness here depends on the compact reduction and exhaustive seed analysis. Different quadratic orders, norms and unit groups require their own reduction argument.

## Extension

Intersect the two orbits with modular conditions by computing the unit action on (Z/mZ)²; use the resulting finite cycles to obtain exact admissible exponent classes and bounded rank/select operations.
