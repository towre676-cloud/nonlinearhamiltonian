## Revised question retained in the annual source

Which exact algebraic certificate can discharge an irreducibility task, and what additional theorem is needed before a curve classification follows?

## Summary

The recovered October workload distinguishes curve modules, irreducible equations and point-carrying cases. These are different mathematical obligations. A reusable reduction certificate can settle a polynomial irreducibility statement, while point completeness still requires the actual arithmetic argument. The examples here are explicitly chosen models, not reconstructions of the unavailable positive-k equations.

## Definitions

A polynomial f in Z[x] is primitive when its coefficients have gcd one. Reduction modulo a prime p preserves its degree if p does not divide the leading coefficient.

Irreducibility is over a specified field. A rational-point completeness theorem asserts that every rational point satisfying the stated curve equation occurs in a specified list or parametrization.

## Result name

A sound finite-field route and its scope

## Result statement

If a primitive f in Z[x] has degree-preserving irreducible reduction modulo p, then f is irreducible over Q. For degree two or three, irreducibility over F_p is equivalent to having no root in F_p. Neither statement supplies all rational points on an associated curve.

## Proof

Gauss’s lemma turns a nontrivial rational factorization into f=gh with primitive integer factors of positive degree. Because the leading coefficient of f is nonzero modulo p, both leading coefficients of g and h are nonzero modulo p. Reduction therefore preserves their positive degrees and contradicts irreducibility of the reduction. For a quadratic or cubic over a field, any nontrivial factorization has a linear factor, equivalent to a root; conversely a root gives a linear divisor. In higher degree, rootlessness alone is insufficient because both factors may have degree at least two. This proves a precise certificate rule with no appeal to numeric root approximations. The logical separation from point classification is visible already in a hyperelliptic equation y²=f(x): irreducibility of f does not determine which rational x make f(x) a square.

## Worked example: setup

Use f=x³−x+1 and contrast a fourth-degree polynomial.

## Worked example: calculation

Modulo two, f becomes x³+x+1. Its values at 0 and 1 are both 1, so it is an irreducible cubic and f is irreducible over Q. The curve y²=x³−x+1 has the easily checked rational points (0,±1), (1,±1) and (3,±5). Irreducibility alone neither produces these points nor proves that any proposed finite list is complete. For a sharper algebraic caution, x⁴+1 factors modulo three as (x²+x+2)(x²+2x+2), although it is irreducible over Q: after x↦x+1 it becomes x⁴+4x³+6x²+4x+2, which is Eisenstein at two. A failed reduction test is therefore inconclusive.
