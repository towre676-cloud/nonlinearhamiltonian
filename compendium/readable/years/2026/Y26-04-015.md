## Revised question retained in the annual source

Which relation between a power-spectrum slope and Hurst exponent is valid for the chosen process?

## Status

Developed classical result with a checked example; recovered claims corrected where needed

## Summary

The source records S(f)∼f^(−β) and a formula for H without identifying the underlying stochastic process. Different integration conventions shift spectral exponents by two. A precise fractional-noise model supplies the standard relation and a clear calibration example.

## Definitions

Let B_H be fractional Brownian motion with covariance E[B_H(t)B_H(s)]=( |t|^(2H)+|s|^(2H)−|t−s|^(2H))/2, 0<H<1.

Its stationary unit increments X_n=B_H(n+1)−B_H(n) are fractional Gaussian noise. For 1/2<H<1, their long-lag covariance is asymptotic to H(2H−1)|k|^(2H−2).

## Result name

Noise and integrated spectral exponents

## Result statement

For fractional Gaussian noise in the long-memory range, S(f)∼C|f|^(1−2H), so β=2H−1 and H=(β+1)/2. Formal integration multiplies the spectrum by |f|⁻², producing exponent 2H+1 for the nonstationary fractional-Brownian convention.

## Proof

Compute the increment covariance from the displayed B_H covariance: γ(k)=((k+1)^(2H)−2k^(2H)+(k−1)^(2H))/2. A Taylor expansion gives the asserted k^(2H−2) tail. The Fourier transform of a regularly varying covariance with exponent between −1 and zero has exponent 1−2H near zero; equivalently rescale the oscillatory integral ∫t^(2H−2)cos(ft)dt. Applying the discrete integration relation X_n=Y_(n+1)−Y_n multiplies the spectral density by |e^(if)−1|²∼f², explaining the exponent shift. Stationarity must not be assumed for B_H itself.

## Worked example: setup

Use ordinary Brownian motion H=1/2.

## Worked example: calculation

Its unit increments are independent, so the stationary noise spectrum is flat and β=0. The integrated random-walk convention has a low-frequency f⁻² profile. These two calibrations already rule out applying one universal H=(2−β)/2 formula to both objects.

## Worked example: conclusion

Spectral color becomes mathematically interpretable only after the process and integration level are declared.
