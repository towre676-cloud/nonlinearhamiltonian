## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: What exact unit consequence follows from a signed corner equation, and how do its six cross-ratio transforms behave?

## Status

Classical theorem or newly stipulated exact model; speculative source assertions delimited

## Summary

The external research program proposes corner equations and several variants. We give a useful elementary arithmetic theorem valid for positive exponents. It yields a finite orbit of exceptional units but avoids assuming that six written formulas produce six distinct field elements.

## Definitions

Let z be an algebraic integer and suppose (1−z)^a=εz^b, where a, b are positive integers and ε is an algebraic unit.

The anharmonic transforms are z,1−z,1/z,1/(1−z), z/(z−1),(z−1)/z, defined for z≠0,1.

## Result name

Corner pins generate exceptional units and a finite anharmonic orbit

## Result statement

z and1−z are units. Every anharmonic transform is also an exceptional unit and lies in the same number field. The orbit has at most six elements and can be smaller at special values.

## Proof

The principal ideals(z) and(1−z) are coprime because their sum contains z+(1−z)=1. The corner equation says(1−z)^a=(z)^b as ideals, since ε is a unit. If a nonzero prime ideal divided(z), it would divide the right side and hence(1−z), contradicting coprimality. Thus(z) is the unit ideal, and similarly for1−z. This proves both are units. Their inverses and quotients are therefore algebraic integers and units. For each anharmonic transform w, its complement1−w is again a signed quotient or inverse of z and1−z; for example1−1/z=(z−1)/z and1−z/(z−1)=−1/(z−1), so it too is a unit. The transformations are generated by z↦1−z and z↦1/z; their elementary composition table has at most six forms, all rationally invertible, proving the same-field and orbit statements. The argument requires positive exponents and integrality, not merely a complex numerical solution.

## Worked example: setup

Take the golden-ratio unit z=φ satisfying(1−z)²=z^{−2}.

## Worked example: calculation

This written relation uses a negative exponent, but multiplication gives z²(1−z)²=1, directly proving both factors are units. Its six transforms reduce to φ,1−φ,φ^{−1},−φ,φ²,φ^{−2}, with possible equality checked algebraically. By contrast z=1/2 has an anharmonic orbit of only three values and is not an algebraic integer unit, showing why the unit hypotheses matter.

## Worked example: conclusion

The corner program gains a rigorous bridge to exceptional-unit arithmetic and an exact finite symmetry.
