## Definitions

A species is a finite nonincreasing list a₁≥⋯≥a_r≥1 of prime exponents. Its representatives are positive integers with that multiset of nonzero exponents. Let p_i denote the i-th prime.

Define the least candidate m(a)=∏_{i=1}^r p_i^{a_i}. Arithmetic invariants are Ω=Σa_i, ω=r and τ=∏(a_i+1).

## Result name

An exponent species has a unique least integer representative

## Result statement

m(a) is the unique least positive integer in its species. Its divisor-count, total-prime-factor and distinct-prime-factor invariants are exactly the stated product and sums.

## Proof

First any chosen r distinct primes q₁<⋯<q_r satisfy q_i≥p_i. Replacing them by the first r primes without changing exponent assignments cannot increase the product and strictly decreases it if any chosen prime differs. Next if p<q but exponents a<b are assigned in the wrong order, compare p^a q^b with p^b q^a: their ratio is (q/p)^{b−a}>1. Swapping the exponents strictly decreases the number. Repeated exchanges put the exponents in descending order on ascending primes. Equality permits only swaps among equal exponents, which do not change the integer, proving uniqueness of the numerical minimizer. Every divisor independently chooses an exponent between zero and a_i, yielding τ; counting factors with and without multiplicity gives Ω and ω.

## Worked example: calculation

The least integer is2³·3²·5=360. Its τ is4·3·2=24, Ω=6 and ω=3. Swapping the first two unequal exponents gives2²·3³·5=540, larger by3/2. Using primes2,3,7 instead gives504, larger by7/5. Exhaustively assigning these exponents to the first few primes verifies the exchange proof’s finite predictions.
