## Revised question retained in the annual source

What local inequality certifies positivity on an entire parameter box, and how should anisotropic boxes be chosen?

## Status

## Summary

The July16–18 continuation used moving centers, Taylor models and adaptive subdivision. A finite-box theorem identifies the essential certificate. It explains both the legitimate advantage of anisotropy and why the reported center values or45-point checks alone cannot prove a continuum claim.

## Definitions

Let f be continuously differentiable on a box centered at c with radii r_i. Suppose an enclosure gives f(c)≥m and uniform coordinate derivative bounds|∂_if|≤L_i. All quantities are rigorous bounds, not point samples.

## Result name

A box positivity certificate and an optimal first-order shape

## Result statement

If m−ΣL_ir_i>0, then f>0 everywhere in the box. With positive L_i and a fixed budgetΣL_ir_i≤M, the unconstrained maximum-volume box has r_i=M/(dL_i).

## Proof

Integrate f along the coordinate-segment path from c to any x in the box. The absolute change is at mostΣL_i|x_i−c_i|≤ΣL_ir_i, proving the lower bound. For volume, set a_i=L_ir_i. AM–GM gives∏a_i≤(M/d)^d, with equality when every a_i=M/d. Thus the longest certified direction is the one with the smallest derivative bound. Higher Taylor models can replace the linear budget, but then their coefficient and remainder enclosures must remain uniform throughout each cell. A union of certified boxes proves the global statement only if it really covers the intended domain and every center’s certificate uses the correct parameter/base point.
