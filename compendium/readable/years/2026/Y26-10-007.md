## Revised question retained in the annual source

Which exact incidence factorization explains invertibility, determinant and solution of a GCD linear system?

## Status

Classical mathematics or a newly chosen finite model developed with proof; historical claims delimited

## Summary

The recovered GCD-kernel discussion has a precise arithmetic factorization. A related cumulative-sum kernel explains why a dense covariance matrix can have an integer tridiagonal inverse. These are separate examples of a common incidence-matrix principle.

## Definitions

Let G_n have entries gcd(i,j), 1≤i,j≤n, and E_ij=1 when j divides i, zero otherwise. Let φ be Euler’s totient.

For the second example, let L_ij=1 when j≤i and K_ij=min(i,j).

## Result name

Exact arithmetic and chain-incidence factorizations

## Result statement

G_n=E diag(φ(1),…,φ(n))Eᵀ, so det G_n=∏_(j=1)^nφ(j)>0. Also K=LLᵀ and K⁻¹=DᵀD, where D has diagonal one and subdiagonal −1. Its diagonal is (2,…,2,1) and its adjacent off-diagonals are −1.

## Proof

The divisor identity Σ_(d|m)φ(d)=m follows by grouping the integers 1,…,m according to their gcd with m. Hence (E diagφ Eᵀ)_ij=Σ_(d|i,d|j)φ(d)=gcd(i,j). E is lower triangular with diagonal one, proving the determinant and positive definiteness. Solving Gx=b reduces to two triangular substitutions and positive scalar divisions; the inverse incidence matrix is the divisibility Möbius matrix, by the identity Σ_(d|m)μ(d)=0 for m>1. For the chain kernel, (LLᵀ)_ij counts indices at most both i and j, giving min(i,j). D=L⁻¹ because first differences invert cumulative sums. Therefore K⁻¹=DᵀD; direct multiplication gives the stated tridiagonal entries. The last diagonal is one because the final coordinate occurs in only the last row of D.

## Worked example: setup

Check the n=3 chain kernel and a GCD determinant.

## Worked example: calculation

K=[[1,1,1],[1,2,2],[1,2,3]] has inverse [[2,−1,0],[−1,2,−1],[0,−1,1]]. The GCD matrix [[1,1,1],[1,2,1],[1,1,3]] has determinant φ(1)φ(2)φ(3)=2. Both identities are verified by exact matrix multiplication.

## Worked example: conclusion

Dense kernels can have sparse inverses because they encode an integrated local difference operator.

## Limits

The min kernel is a newly chosen chain model, not an identification of every unspecified normalized GCD kernel in the source. A factorization over Q does not imply its inverse has integer entries; the totient diagonal generally introduces denominators.

## Extension

Replace gcd(i,j) by f(gcd(i,j)); Möbius inversion gives diagonal weights (f*μ)(d). Positivity of those weights is then an explicit sufficient condition for positive definiteness on the initial divisor-closed set.
