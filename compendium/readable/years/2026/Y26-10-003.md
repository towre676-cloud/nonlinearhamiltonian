## Revised question retained in the annual source

How can the unit part of n! modulo pᵏ be computed without ever dividing a nonunit modulo pᵏ?

## Status

Classical mathematics or a newly chosen finite model developed with proof; historical claims delimited

## Summary

The recovered factorial-unit claim becomes a clean integer recursion. Removing powers of p must occur before modular reduction; division by p after reduction is not legitimate. The same decomposition yields a logarithmic-depth algorithm with exact residue-block products.

## Definitions

For prime p, let v_p(n!)=Σ_(j≥1)⌊n/pʲ⌋ and U_p(n)=n!/p^(v_p(n!)), with U_p(0)=1.

Set m=pᵏ, W=∏_(1≤i≤m,p∤i)i modulo m, and V(r)=∏_(1≤i≤r,p∤i)i modulo m for 0≤r<m.

## Result name

Unit recursion and complete-block reduction

## Result statement

U_p(n)=[∏_(1≤i≤n,p∤i)i]U_p(⌊n/p⌋) as integers. Consequently U_p(n)≡∏_(j≥0)W^(⌊⌊n/pʲ⌋/m⌋)V(⌊n/pʲ⌋ mod m) modulo m, with only finitely many nontrivial factors.

## Proof

Partition the factors of n! into indices not divisible by p and indices i=pj. For the latter, stripping all powers of p leaves j/p^(v_p(j)), precisely the factors defining U_p(⌊n/p⌋). This proves the integer recursion, rather than an invalid modular cancellation. To compute its first factor modulo m, write n=am+r. Each complete interval ℓm+1,…,(ℓ+1)m has the same reduced residues modulo m; because p divides m, it also has exactly the same set of positions not divisible by p. Its unit product is therefore W. The remaining r positions contribute V(r). Iterating with n replaced by its successive p-quotients terminates at zero. The valuation formula follows separately by counting each factor p once for multiples of p, again for multiples of p², and so on. Both claims are finite integer factorizations and need no limiting p-adic argument.

## Worked example: setup

Compute the unit part of 10! modulo nine.

## Worked example: calculation

For p=3, v₃(10!)=3+1=4, so U₃(10)=3628800/81=44800≡7 mod 9. The nonmultiples-of-three product through ten is 22400≡8 mod 9, while U₃(3)=2. Their product gives 8·2≡7 mod 9, independently matching the stripped factorial.

## Worked example: conclusion

The recursion preserves every unit factor and avoids modular division by three.

## Limits

A residue of the unit part is not the factorial itself; the valuation must be retained. Cancellation in a factorial ratio requires combining valuations and inverting only residues coprime to p. Historical sequence-family checks are not replayed merely by proving this recursion.

## Extension

Apply the method to ∏(a_j n+b_j)!^(e_j): compute its exact valuation first, then its unit ratio modulo pᵏ, and state explicitly whether a negative valuation gives a p-adic number rather than an integer.
