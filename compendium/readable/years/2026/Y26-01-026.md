## Revised question retained in the annual source

What exact response formula follows from a deformed finite spectrum, and when do mixtures describe distinct equilibrium sectors?

## Status

Developed mathematical treatment with explicit hypotheses, derivation and checked example

## Summary

Differentiating a finite partition sum gives an exact expectation and a covariance response. A direct-sum algebra then gives an elementary equilibrium mixture, separating sector weights from unsupported scattering corrections or a uniquely selected exponent.

## Definitions

Let E_j>0, c real and β>0. Define ε_j(a)=E_j+cE_j^a, Z(a)=∑_j e^{−βε_j(a)} and Gibbs weights p_j=e^{−βε_j}/Z.

All sums are finite, so differentiation may be interchanged with summation without a convergence theorem.

A block algebra A₁⊕A₂ has central projections distinguishing sectors; a full matrix algebra need not have that sector freedom.

## Result name

Exact parameter response and a sector example

## Result statement

∂_a log Z=−βc E_p[E^a log E]. Its second derivative is β²Var_p(cE^a log E)−β E_p[cE^a(log E)²]. On the commutative algebra C⊕C with trivial dynamics, every state (w,1−w), 0≤w≤1, is KMS and is a mixture of its two extremal states.

## Proof

Differentiate the exponential terms to obtain the first formula. For a general observable f_j(a), differentiation of normalized weights gives d E_p f/da=E_p f′−βCov_p(f,ε′). Apply it to f=ε′ to derive the second derivative. A zero first derivative therefore depends on the actual spectrum, c and β; no universal a is selected by notation. For the sector example all products commute and the dynamics is identity, so the KMS boundary equation reduces to φ(AB)=φ(BA), true for every state. Positivity and normalization reduce to 0≤w≤1. The extreme states w=0,1 and their convex combination give an exact two-sector decomposition.

## Worked example: setup

Use E=(1,4), c=1, a=1/2 and β=log2.

## Worked example: calculation

The deformed energies are (2,6), giving weights (16/17,1/17). Since E^a log E is (0,4log2), the derivative is −4(log2)²/17≈−.113048. It is not zero at a=1/2. The two-state sector mixture can choose weights freely only in the stated direct-sum/trivial-dynamics model, not in this full finite Gibbs problem at fixed Hamiltonian.

## Worked example: conclusion

Every response and mixture has a specified spectrum, state and algebra.

## Limits

Infinite spectral sums require trace-class and dominated-differentiation hypotheses. The historical GL(4) Eisenstein/scattering correction is not proved by a finite partition calculation, and no a=1/4 significance is inherited.

## Extension

For a supplied scattering determinant, establish its spectral measure and convergence domain, derive the continuous-spectrum contribution and justify parameter differentiation. Then test whether a claimed stationary exponent survives the exact correction.
