## Revised question retained in the annual source

When can a state become orthogonal to its earlier value under a self-adjoint Hamiltonian?

## Status

Classical result; recovered proposal repaired

## Summary

The recovered discussion mentions temporal orthogonality but the relevant formula is truncated. A two-dimensional example settles the basic issue: unitarity preserves inner products between jointly evolved states, but does not make a trajectory orthogonal to itself at every later time.

## Definitions

For a self-adjoint finite-dimensional Hamiltonian H and ℏ=1, U(t)=e^(−itH).

The survival amplitude of a unit initial vector ψ is c(t)=〈ψ,U(t)ψ〉; orthogonality means c(t)=0.

## Result name

An exact two-level orthogonality criterion

## Result statement

For H=diag(1,−1) and ψ=(1,1)/√2, c(t)=cos t. Orthogonality occurs exactly at t=π/2+kπ, not at arbitrary nonzero time steps.

## Proof

Diagonal exponentiation gives U(t)=diag(e^(−it),e^(it)). Therefore c(t)=(e^(−it)+e^(it))/2=cos t. Its zeros are precisely the stated times. More generally, if ψ=Σ_jc_j e_j in an orthonormal eigenbasis, then c(t)=Σ_j|c_j|²e^(−itE_j). Thus the overlap is the Fourier transform of a probability measure on energies. If ψ is an eigenstate, the amplitude has modulus1 for every time and never vanishes. Also c(0)=1 and continuity ensures that every fixed finite-dimensional state has a nonzero overlap with itself for all sufficiently small time increments. A global phase multiplying a state changes its vector representative but not its ray or the modulus of its overlap. The eigenstate example makes this distinction exact: the vector rotates by e^(−it), yet its survival probability remains1. Orthogonality is invariant under those phase choices.

## Worked example: setup

Compare the equal superposition ψ above with the eigenstate e1.

## Worked example: calculation

For ψ, |c(π/4)|²=1/2 and |c(π/2)|²=0. For e1, c(t)=e^(−it) and |c(t)|²=1. The difference is entirely determined by the energy distribution, even though both evolutions are unitary under the same H.

## Worked example: conclusion

Orthogonality is a special cancellation condition in the energy phases. It cannot be imposed as a universal property of continuous unitary evolution.

## Limits

This example does not derive a general quantum speed-limit theorem. Unbounded operators require a self-adjoint domain and additional moment assumptions if derivatives of c(t) are used. The truncated historical source cannot support a more specific original theorem.

## Extension

Given a finite energy distribution, bound the earliest zero using its variance or spectral width, and state the equality cases. Such a bound is a meaningful replacement for an unsupported all-times orthogonality claim.
