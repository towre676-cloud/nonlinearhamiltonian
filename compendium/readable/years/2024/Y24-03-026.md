## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: Under which analyticity and orientation assumptions does a contour integral recover a derivative, and what exact integrals result for a polynomial and an exponential?

## Status

Classical mathematics newly developed from a recovered assistant-only catalog name

## Summary

A contour formula is incomplete without its enclosed point, orientation and analytic domain. With those specified, Cauchy’s formula turns a seemingly difficult complex integral into derivative evaluation. The formula also yields a useful derivative bound, so the same theorem supports both exact calculation and error control.

## Definitions

Let f be holomorphic on an open set containing the closed disk |z−a|≤r, with r>0. Let C be its counterclockwise circular boundary.

For an integer n≥0, Cauchy’s formula reads f^(n)(a)=n!/(2πi)∮_C f(z)/(z−a)^(n+1) dz. More general simple closed curves work when the same analyticity and interior conditions hold.

A line integral uses a parameterization z(t) and the factor z′(t)dt. Omitting that factor or reversing the orientation changes the calculation.

## Result name

Boundary data recover derivatives and control their size

## Result statement

Under the stated hypotheses, the derivative formula holds and |f^(n)(a)|≤n!M/rⁿ, where M=max_C|f|. In particular ∮_|z|=2 (z⁴+2z)/(z−1)³ dz=12πi and ∮_|z|=2 eᶻ/(z−1)³ dz=πie.

## Proof

For n=0, Cauchy’s theorem on the annulus between C and a small circle around a equates the two integrals of f(z)/(z−a). Parameterizing the small circle as a+εe^(it) makes its integral i∫₀^(2π)f(a+εe^(it))dt, which tends to 2πif(a) by uniform continuity. This proves the base formula using Cauchy’s theorem as the classical analytic input. For an interior variable a, differentiate under the boundary integral; the denominator stays uniformly separated from zero on a sufficiently small neighborhood. Repeating n times supplies n!/(z−a)^(n+1). The bound follows by taking absolute values, using contour length 2πr and denominator modulus r^(n+1). In the first example f″(1)=12, and the derivative formula multiplies this by 2πi/2!=πi. In the second f″(1)=e, giving πie. The pole at one is strictly inside |z|=2 and neither numerator has another singularity.

## Worked example: setup

Compute the two displayed integrals on z(t)=2e^(it), 0≤t≤2π.

## Worked example: calculation

For the polynomial, expanding z⁴+2z around z=1 gives coefficient six on (z−1)², so division by (z−1)³ has residue six and integral 12πi. For eᶻ, the corresponding coefficient is e/2 and integral πie. A periodic trapezoidal quadrature with 256 points independently approximates each integral and is compared with the analytic answers. That numerical agreement is a finite check; the coefficient and contour arguments give the proof.

## Worked example: conclusion

Both examples fix the factorial, orientation and 2πi normalization, while the bound shows what analyticity additionally controls.

## Limits

A contour passing through a singularity is outside these hypotheses. Merely having a real derivative on a curve does not imply complex analyticity inside it. The numerical quadrature is not used as a substitute for those conditions.

## Extension

If f is bounded by M on a disk of radius R, the Taylor remainder at |z−a|=ρ<R is bounded by M(ρ/R)^(N+1)/(1−ρ/R). This follows from the coefficient bounds and a geometric series, giving a directly usable analytic approximation certificate.
