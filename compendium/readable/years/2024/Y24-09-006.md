## Revised question retained in the annual source

Which coefficient bounds make the angular series continuous or differentiable, before any geometric interpretation is attempted?

## Status

Classical result; recovered proposal repaired

## Summary

A meaningful infinite angular construction needs a convergence theorem. The same coefficients that control its size do not necessarily control its slopes: each differentiation multiplies the nth oscillation by ξ^n. This gives an explicit hierarchy of sufficient conditions.

## Definitions

Let ξ>1 and r(θ)=1+Σ_{n≥0}a_n cos(ξ^nθ).

For k≥0, put M_k=Σ_{n≥0}|a_n|ξ^(kn), allowing the value infinity.

## Result name

A sufficient regularity criterion

## Result statement

If M_k is finite for an integer k≥0, the series defines a C^k function on R. Every derivative of order j≤k is obtained term by term and is uniformly bounded by M_j.

## Proof

The jth derivative of a_n cos(ξ^nθ) has absolute value at most |a_n|ξ^(jn). Since ξ>1 and j≤k, finiteness of M_k implies finiteness of M_j. The Weierstrass uniform convergence test therefore applies to every derivative series through order k. To justify differentiation, fix a base point and integrate the uniformly convergent first derivative series: the fundamental theorem of calculus identifies its integral with the uniform limit of the original partial sums. Repeating this argument proves the result through order k. The criterion is sufficient only. Failure of absolute summability of derivative bounds does not by itself prove nondifferentiability, because cancellation can matter. For k=0 the uniform tail bound is sup|Σ_(n>N)a_n cos(ξ^nθ)|≤Σ_(n>N)|a_n|. In the first geometric example this equals(1/16)(1/8)^(N+1)/(1−1/8). Thus every plotted truncation has a certified amplitude error, independently of sampling density on the angular line.

## Worked example: setup

Choose a_n=2^(−3n−4), ξ=2, and n starting at0. Compare it with a_n=2^(−n−4).

## Worked example: calculation

For the first choice, M_0=(1/16)/(1−1/8)=1/14 and M_2=(1/16)/(1−1/2)=1/8. Hence r≥13/14 and r is C² with |r″|≤1/8. For the second, M_0=1/8 but the first derivative majorant is Σ1/16, which diverges; the theorem guarantees continuity but supplies no C¹ conclusion.

## Worked example: conclusion

The first series is a quantitatively controlled positive radial function. The second needs additional analysis before slopes or curvature can be used.

## Limits

For noninteger ξ the resulting function still need not descend to S¹. Positivity and regularity do not repair missing periodicity. No fractal dimension is inferred from the divergence of a sufficient derivative bound.

## Extension

Once periodicity is established, combine the derivative bounds with an explicit curvature formula for a polar graph to identify a rigorously convex parameter range.
