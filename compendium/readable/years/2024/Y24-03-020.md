## Summary

A transform formula needs a domain of convergence and a rule for boundary terms. Those conditions explain why the initial value appears when a differential equation becomes an algebraic equation. The recovered passage explicitly gives the one-sided integral; the following initial-value problem and its analysis are newly chosen.

## Definitions

For locally integrable f on [0,∞), define F(s)=∫₀∞e^(−st)f(t)dt wherever the integral converges absolutely. The variable s may be complex.

A sufficient condition is |f(t)|≤Me^(αt) for all sufficiently large t; then absolute convergence holds for Re(s)>α. Assume f is locally absolutely continuous when transforming f′, and assume both f and f′ have suitable exponential bounds.

For two causal functions define (f*g)(t)=∫₀ᵗf(τ)g(t−τ)dτ. Absolute integrability after a common exponential weight justifies changing the order of integration.

## Result name

Derivative and convolution identities with their boundary conditions

## Result statement

For Re(s) sufficiently large, L[f′](s)=sF(s)−f(0) and L[f*g](s)=F(s)G(s). Consequently y′+2y=1, y(0)=0 has y(t)=(1−e^(−2t))/2 for t≥0.

## Proof

Integrating e^(−st)f′ by parts on [0,T] gives e^(−sT)f(T)−f(0)+s∫₀ᵀe^(−st)f(t)dt. The exponential bound makes the first term vanish as T→∞ when Re(s)>α. For convolution, set t=τ+u in the triangular integration region. Fubini then factors ∫₀∞∫₀∞e^(−sτ)f(τ)e^(−su)g(u)dudτ into F(s)G(s). In the chosen equation the transformed constant is 1/s and the derivative identity yields (s+2)Y(s)=1/s, so Y(s)=1/[s(s+2)]=(1/2)(1/s−1/(s+2)). Direct integration verifies the displayed inverse. Existence can also be checked by substitution, and uniqueness does not need a transform inversion theorem here: the difference of two solutions satisfies z′+2z=0 and z(0)=0, hence e^(2t)z(t) is identically zero.

## Limits

These are sufficient regularity hypotheses, not the most general transform theory. An absolutely convergent Laplace integral need not converge on its boundary line. No inversion claim is made for arbitrary functions outside the stated class.

## Extension

For an input u(t), the same equation has y(t)=e^(−2t)y(0)+(e^(−2·)*u)(t). This makes the impulse-response kernel explicit and supplies the bound |y(t)|≤|y(0)|e^(−2t)+||u||∞(1−e^(−2t))/2 for bounded inputs.
