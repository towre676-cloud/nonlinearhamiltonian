## Definitions

For real k>0, the two-dimensional Helmholtz equation is Δu+k²u=0. In polar coordinates away from r=0, Δ=∂ᵣᵣ+r^(−1)∂ᵣ+r^(−2)∂θθ.

For an integer m≥0, Jₘ(z)=Σⱼ≥₀(−1)ʲ(z/2)^(2j+m)/[j!(j+m)!]. Its series converges for every complex z.

A separated angular mode e^(imθ) is single valued because m is an integer. A bounded smooth solution at the disk center uses the regular radial mode Jₘ(kr), rather than the singular second solution.

## Result name

Separation, coefficient recurrence and a concrete Helmholtz mode

## Result statement

Substituting u=R(r)e^(imθ) into Helmholtz yields r²R″+rR′+(k²r²−m²)R=0. With z=kr this is Bessel’s equation, and Jₘ is its regular series solution. Independently, sin(3x)sin(4y) solves Δu+25u=0.

## Proof

Differentiating the separated product and cancelling the nonzero angular factor gives the radial equation directly. For R=Σⱼcⱼz^(m+2j), the coefficient of z^(m+2j) in z²R″+zR′−m²R+z²R is 4j(m+j)cⱼ+cⱼ₋₁ for j≥1. Therefore cⱼ=−cⱼ₋₁/[4j(m+j)], which is exactly the displayed Jₘ coefficient sequence after choosing c₀=1/(2ᵐm!). The ratio test proves convergence on every compact set and justifies termwise differentiation. For the Cartesian example, ∂ₓₓu=−9u and ∂ᵧᵧu=−16u, so the residual is zero. On the rectangle (0,π/3)×(0,π/4) the same function vanishes on all four sides. On a disk of radius a, an analogous regular Dirichlet mode must instead satisfy Jₘ(ka)=0; that boundary condition is an extra spectral restriction, not part of the differential equation alone.
