## Summary

## Definitions

Let x,y be smooth paths on [0,T] with fixed endpoints, and let ξ,η be independent smooth variations vanishing there.

The Euler–Lagrange residuals are E_x=L_x−d(L_ẋ)/dt and E_y=L_y−d(L_ẏ)/dt.

A weighting symbol must have a defined domain and value. It cannot replace the independent test functions required by a variational principle.

## Result name

Independent variations give separate Euler–Lagrange equations

## Result statement

Stationarity under every pair (ξ,η) is equivalent to the two residual equations. Stationarity only along η=κξ generally imposes just E_x+κE_y=0 and can miss genuine directions.

## Proof

Differentiate S[x+εξ,y+εη] at ε=0 and integrate the velocity terms by parts. Endpoint terms vanish, leaving δS=∫(E_xξ+E_yη)dt. First choose η=0 and arbitrary ξ, then ξ=0 and arbitrary η. The fundamental lemma gives each residual zero. If variations are restricted to η=κξ, the same reasoning gives only their weighted sum. This is a different constrained problem. In finite dimensions, S(a,b)=a−b has gradient (1,−1). Its derivative along the diagonal direction (1,1) vanishes everywhere, although it has no unconstrained stationary point. This simple counterexample disproves the idea that one cancelling sum certifies all variations.

Take L=(ẋ²+κẏ²)/2 with fixed endpoints.

The equations are ẍ=0 and κÿ=0. If κ≠0, both paths are affine interpolants. At κ=0, the functional sees no y variation and every admissible y has the same contribution. The rank loss is an exact parameter stratum, not evidence that an undefined geometric coefficient creates an extra field law.

## Worked example: conclusion

The revised model separates a variational derivation, a constraint on variations and a degeneracy of the functional.

## Limits

The calculation assumes sufficient regularity for differentiation and integration by parts. Weak solutions require a specified function space. Stationarity is not automatically minimality; the second variation must be checked.

## Extension

For the oscillator action ∫(q̇²−ω²q²)/2, test q=εsin(πt/T). Its action is ε²T[(π/T)²−ω²]/4, negative when T>π/ω. This explicitly shows why the phrase least action must be handled as stationary action. For T<π/ω, the Poincaré inequality ∫q̇²≥(π/T)²∫q² proves positivity of the quadratic action for all zero-endpoint paths. The same time threshold therefore marks a real change in the second variation.
