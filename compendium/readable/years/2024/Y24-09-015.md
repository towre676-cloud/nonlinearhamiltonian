## Revised question retained in the annual source

What theorem would justify a leading-digit law, and why does a coefficient deformation not supply it?

## Status

Classical result; recovered proposal repaired

## Summary

The September 10 assistant claim gives no usable definition or asymptotic proof of a deformed Benford law for elliptic coefficients. A sound formulation reduces leading digits to fractional logarithms and proves an exact model where equidistribution is available.

## Definitions

For x>0, its base10 leading digit is d when the fractional part of log10x lies in[log10d,log10(d+1)).

For signed coefficients use |a_n|; zero coefficients must be excluded or counted in a separately specified category.

## Result name

A sufficient criterion and a proved model

## Result statement

If the fractional parts of log10x_n are uniformly distributed on[0,1), then digit d has limiting frequency log10(1+1/d). For x_n=10^(nθ), every irrational θ satisfies this criterion.

## Proof

The digit event is precisely the interval in the definition, whose length is log10(d+1)−log10d. Uniform distribution gives its limiting frequency. For the model sequence and every nonzero integer k, the exponential average is N⁻¹Σ_(n=1)^N exp(2πiknθ). It is a geometric sum with ratio q=exp(2πikθ)≠1. Its magnitude is at most2/(N|1−q|), which tends to zero. Weyl’s criterion therefore yields uniform distribution. The nonvanishing denominator is where irrationality is used. In contrast, x_n=10^n always begins with1; rapid growth alone is insufficient. A nonlinear change of the index or coefficients needs a new equidistribution argument, because it changes the logarithmic sequence. The exponential-sum estimate depends onk through|1−exp(2πikθ)|. It proves convergence for every fixed nonzerok but does not give a uniform discrepancy bound over allk. A quantitative digit error estimate needs additional Diophantine control or an explicit finite-frequency approximation of the interval indicator.

## Worked example: setup

Take θ=√2 and compare x_n=10^(n√2) with y_n=10^n. No huge numbers need be formed.

## Worked example: calculation

For x_n compute only {n√2}; the limiting probability of first digit1 is log10 2≈0.30103 and of digit9 is log10(10/9)≈0.04576. For y_n every fractional logarithm is0, so digit1 has frequency1. A finite program can count the first10000 logarithmic fractions reproducibly, but the geometric-sum proof supplies the actual limiting conclusion.

## Worked example: conclusion

Benford behavior is an equidistribution theorem, not a generic consequence of attaching an exponent or a star to an arithmetic sequence.

## Limits

This does not establish any Benford law for elliptic-curve coefficients. Their zeros, multiplicativity, normalization and sampling over all integers versus primes are substantive choices. No historical original user question was recovered for this claim.

## Extension

Define the intended sampling set and transformed coefficients exactly, then study exponential sums of their logarithms. Until such bounds exist, retain the digit histogram as a finite descriptive computation.
