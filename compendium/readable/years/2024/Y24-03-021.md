## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: How does Rodrigues’ formula force Legendre orthogonality, and what is the exact projection of x² onto its first three modes?

## Status

## Summary

Legendre polynomials are useful because a concrete derivative construction produces a basis adapted to integration on [−1,1]. The improvement over a bare formula is to connect normalization, orthogonality and coefficient extraction. Everything beyond the recovered catalog name is a new classical development.

## Definitions

For an integer n≥0, set Pₙ(x)=(1/(2ⁿn!))dⁿ/dxⁿ[(x²−1)ⁿ]. This normalization gives Pₙ(1)=1.

Use the real inner product ⟨f,g⟩=∫₋₁¹f(x)g(x)dx. Orthogonality means ⟨Pₙ,Pₘ⟩=0 when n≠m.

The leading coefficient of Pₙ is cₙ=(2n)!/[2ⁿ(n!)²], so P₀,…,Pₙ form a basis of polynomials of degree at most n.

## Result name

Rodrigues orthogonality and the exact norm

## Result statement

For every polynomial q of degree less than n, ∫₋₁¹Pₙq=0. Moreover ∫₋₁¹Pₙ²dx=2/(2n+1), so the Legendre coefficient of f is aₙ=((2n+1)/2)∫₋₁¹fPₙ when this integral exists.

## Proof

The factor (x²−1)ⁿ has zeros of order n at both endpoints. Integrating by parts n times therefore kills every boundary term and transfers all n derivatives to q; the resulting integral is zero because q^(n)=0. For the norm, write Pₙ=cₙxⁿ+q with deg(q)<n, so ⟨Pₙ,Pₙ⟩=cₙ⟨Pₙ,xⁿ⟩. The same integration-by-parts argument gives ⟨Pₙ,xⁿ⟩=2^(−n)∫₋₁¹(1−x²)ⁿdx. Set Iₙ equal to that last integral. A single integration by parts, or the substitution x=sin θ, gives I₀=2 and Iₙ=(2n/(2n+1))Iₙ₋₁. Thus Iₙ=2^(2n+1)(n!)²/(2n+1)!, and multiplying by cₙ/2ⁿ proves the norm formula. Orthogonality and nonzero norms then make the projection coefficient formula immediate. For a finite polynomial this is exact basis expansion, without any infinite-series convergence issue.

## Worked example: setup

Expand f(x)=x² and evaluate its squared L² norm by orthogonality.

## Worked example: calculation

Rodrigues gives P₀=1, P₁=x and P₂=(3x²−1)/2, hence x²=(1/3)P₀+(2/3)P₂. The expansion predicts ∫₋₁¹x⁴dx=(1/9)·2+(4/9)·(2/5)=2/5, matching direct integration. The verifier constructs P₀ through P₆ with rational coefficients and checks their pairwise inner products exactly. If only a constant approximation is permitted, the best L² constant is 1/3. Orthogonality splits the error into a nonnegative constant-coefficient error plus the unavoidable squared residual 8/45, so this minimizer is unique.

## Worked example: conclusion

A derivative formula becomes a complete small spectral calculation, including the normalization factors that are easily lost in a formula catalog.

## Limits

The finite polynomial arguments prove no pointwise convergence theorem for an arbitrary infinite Legendre series. For general functions one should separately specify an L² space and the intended mode of convergence.

## Extension

The same integration-by-parts structure gives the Sturm–Liouville equation ((1−x²)Pₙ′)′+n(n+1)Pₙ=0. It explains why distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal under the stated endpoint behavior.
