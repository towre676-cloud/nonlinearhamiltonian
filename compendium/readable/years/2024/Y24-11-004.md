## Revised question retained in the annual source

Where does a finite Euler product approximate ζ, and what does perturbing a prime factor measure?

## Status

Developed classical result with a checked example; historical claims corrected where needed

## Summary

The recovered experiment evaluated ordinary partial Euler products on the critical line and interpreted small values as evidence for zeros. The product’s justified domain and its exact factor sensitivity resolve the issue before any high-precision computation.

## Definitions

For Re(s)=σ>1 define Z_P(s)=∏_{p<P}(1−p^(−s))^(−1).

Use p^(−s)=exp(−s log p) with the real logarithm of the positive prime p.

## Result name

Absolute convergence and a finite-factor perturbation identity

## Result statement

For σ>1 the infinite Euler product converges to ζ(s) and is nonzero. Replacing one factor z=p^(−s) by (1+δ)z changes the finite product by the exact ratio (1−z)/(1−(1+δ)z)=1/[1−δz/(1−z)], whenever the denominator is nonzero.

## Proof

Expand each factor as its absolutely convergent geometric series. Unique factorization identifies the resulting sum with Σn^(−s), and absolute convergence justifies rearrangement. Alternatively Σ_pΣ_k|p^(−ks)|/k is finite because it is bounded by a constant times Σ_{n≥2}n^(−σ); its exponential gives a nonzero product. The perturbation formula follows by cancelling every unchanged factor, then algebraically factoring the denominator. Its derivative at δ=0 is z/(1−z), so the conditioning depends on the complex phase as well as the size of p. These arguments do not extend absolute Euler-product convergence to σ=1/2. Analytic continuation of ζ is a different construction.

## Worked example: setup

At s=2 perturb the p=2 factor and compare with the full product. Then examine any finite product at s=1/2+it.

## Worked example: calculation

Here z=1/4 and the ratio is 1/(1−δ/3); the first-order relative change is δ/3. At any point with Re(s)>0 every |p^(−s)|<1, so each finite factor is finite and nonzero and consequently the finite product cannot be a zero of ζ. A numerical value near zero is not a zero certificate.

## Worked example: conclusion

The original critical-line experiment had no established convergence argument and cannot prove the Riemann hypothesis or even locate a zero reliably by ordinary Euler products.

## Limits

This does not rule out suitably regularized products, explicit formulas, or Euler–Hadamard hybrid approximations; each needs its own theorem and error bounds.

## Extension

For σ>1, bound the omitted logarithmic tail by Σ_{n≥P}n^(−σ)/(1−2^(−σ)), and compare that rigorous error with the computed value.
