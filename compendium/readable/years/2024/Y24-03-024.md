## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: How does the Euclidean algorithm certify a greatest common divisor, and how does multiplication of quadratic units generate and classify the positive solutions of x²−2y²=1?

## Status

## Summary

These arithmetic concepts are connected by explicit certificates rather than a shared formula. The Euclidean algorithm produces a finite integer identity; Pell’s equation supplies an infinite family whose recurrence preserves a norm. A descent argument is included so that the chosen Pell example is more than a list of numerically observed solutions.

## Definitions

For integers a,b not both zero, gcd(a,b) is the positive generator of their common-divisor ideal. Bézout coefficients u,v satisfy ua+vb=gcd(a,b).

For the specific Pell equation x²−2y²=1 use integers x,y≥0. Multiplication in Z[√2] preserves the norm N(x+y√2)=x²−2y².

The unit 3+2√2 has norm one. Its powers define x₀=1,y₀=0 and xₙ₊₁=3xₙ+4yₙ, yₙ₊₁=2xₙ+3yₙ.

## Result name

A terminating gcd algorithm and all nonnegative solutions for D=2

## Result statement

Replacing (a,b) by (b,a−qb) preserves common divisors; choosing the remainder 0≤r<|b| ensures termination. For Pell D=2, every solution x,y≥0 is x+y√2=(3+2√2)ⁿ for a unique n≥0.

## Proof

For the gcd claim, any divisor of a,b divides b,a−qb, and conversely a=(a−qb)+qb. Positive remainders strictly decrease, so the last nonzero remainder is the gcd; back-substitution yields Bézout coefficients. For Pell, norm multiplication first proves that all stated powers are solutions. Conversely take any solution with y>0. If y=1, x²=3 is impossible. For y≥2, x/y=√(2+1/y²)≤3/2 and exceeds √2. Multiplying by 3−2√2 gives x′=3x−4y>0 and y′=3y−2x≥0. Moreover y′<y because x>y. The new pair remains integral with norm one, so this descent terminates at y=0, x=1. Reversing every step expresses the original pair as a power of 3+2√2. The exponent is unique because the positive real powers strictly increase. This proves the complete classification for this one value of D, without asserting a general fundamental-unit algorithm.

The remainders are 10,6,4,2,0, and back-substitution gives 2=−9·240+47·46. The Pell recurrence gives (3,2),(17,12),(99,70),(577,408); their norm-one identities are exact. For example 577²−2·408²=332929−332928=1. The verifier checks the Bézout identity, the recurrence and norm through twenty steps, and the descent for the same generated pairs.

## Worked example: conclusion

Finite integer arithmetic yields independently inspectable certificates. The written descent, rather than finite sampling, establishes that no nonnegative D=2 solutions are missing.

## Limits

The Pell classification is explicitly restricted to D=2 and nonnegative x,y; signs produce the other integer solutions. For square D the equation has a different character, and the general nonsquare case requires an additional existence and fundamental-unit argument.

## Extension

The error in xₙ/yₙ as an approximation to √2 is exactly 1/[yₙ²(xₙ/yₙ+√2)] for yₙ>0. Thus the same norm certificate gives a quantitative rational-approximation bound without subtracting nearly equal floating-point numbers.
