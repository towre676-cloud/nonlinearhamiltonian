## Revised question retained in the annual source

How do cross-sections and projections of a higher-dimensional object differ mathematically?

## Status

Developed classical result with a checked example; historical claims corrected where needed

## Summary

The recovered geometry discussion used higher-dimensional objects to motivate real-world inference. A concrete four-dimensional ball shows exactly what observations preserve and what they cannot determine. A slice fixes a coordinate; a projection forgets it.

## Definitions

Let B⁴_R={(x,w)∈R³×R: ||x||²+w²≤R²}.

The slice at w=t intersects a hyperplane; the projection π(x,w)=x maps to R³.

## Result name

Exact slice and fiber formulas

## Result statement

For |t|≤R the slice of B⁴_R is a three-ball of radius √(R²−t²), while its projection is the full three-ball B³_R. For ||x||<R the projection fiber is an interval of length 2√(R²−||x||²).

## Proof

Fixing w=t converts the defining inequality into ||x||²≤R²−t², proving the slice formula and showing the slice is empty for |t|>R. For a projection point x, a suitable w exists exactly when ||x||≤R, because w=0 is then allowed. Solving the inequality for w gives the fiber interval. On the boundary sphere S³_R, a generic interior point of B³_R has two preimages w=±√(R²−||x||²), which merge at ||x||=R. Thus a smooth higher-dimensional manifold can project with a critical boundary or fold; the projected set is not a faithful copy of the original geometry. Inferring hidden dimension from an image requires additional assumptions on fibers and measurement maps.

## Worked example: setup

Take R=2 and slices w=0,√3,2. Compare their volumes and the full projection.

## Worked example: calculation

The slice radii are 2,1,0, with three-volumes 32π/3,4π/3,0 respectively. All slices together project to the radius-two three-ball, but no single generic slice equals the original four-ball. Fubini’s theorem recovers the four-volume by integrating (4π/3)(R²−t²)^(3/2) from −R to R, yielding π²R⁴/2.

## Worked example: conclusion

Slicing, projecting, and reconstructing are different operations with explicitly different outputs.

## Limits

The toy model proves no biological, material, or computational claim. A projection’s apparent singularity need not be a singularity of its smooth source, while singular sources may also project smoothly.

## Extension

For a linear projection of a polytope, eliminate coordinates by linear programming; for nonlinear varieties, compare critical values with constructible-image descriptions.
