## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: Under the creeping-flow assumptions, where does the coefficient 6π in spherical drag come from, and how can terminal speed and Reynolds number be checked consistently?

## Status

Classical mathematics newly developed from a recovered assistant-only catalog name

## Summary

Dimensions suggest drag proportional to viscosity, radius and speed, but cannot determine its numerical coefficient. That coefficient comes from the no-slip exterior Stokes solution and integration of its surface traction. A constructed settling example then checks whether the low-Reynolds-number hypothesis is internally consistent.

## Definitions

Assume a rigid sphere of radius a>0 in an unbounded incompressible Newtonian fluid of dynamic viscosity μ>0, with no slip and steady negligible inertia. The equations are ∇·u=0 and −∇p+μΔu=0.

In the sphere frame let the uniform far velocity be Ue_z. The standard Stokes solution is u_r=Ucosθ(1−3a/(2r)+a³/(2r³)), u_θ=−Usinθ(1−3a/(4r)−a³/(4r³)), p−p∞=−3μUa cosθ/(2r²).

Fluid stress is σ=−pI+μ(∇u+∇uᵀ). The traction integrated on the sphere gives the force exerted by the fluid. For a sphere translating at V relative to still fluid, set U=−V.

## Result name

The surface integral and its low-Reynolds-number consequence

## Result statement

The force on a translating sphere is F=−6πμaV. If its density exceeds the fluid density by Δρ>0 and gravity magnitude is g, the model terminal speed is V_t=2a²Δρg/(9μ). Its applicability must be checked using Re=2aρ_fV_t/μ≪1.

## Proof

Use the stated classical exterior solution as input; it satisfies no slip at r=a and tends to the chosen uniform flow at infinity. At the surface ∂_ru_r=0, so after subtracting the irrelevant constant pressure, σ_rr=(3μU/(2a))cosθ. The shear component is σ_rθ=μ(∂_ru_θ−u_θ/r+r^(−1)∂_θu_r)=−(3μU/(2a))sinθ there. The z component of traction is σ_rrcosθ−σ_rθsinθ=3μU/(2a), independent of θ. Integrating over area 4πa² gives 6πμaU. Equivalently the pressure contributes 2πμaU and shear contributes 4πμaU. Switching from incoming fluid velocity to body velocity reverses the sign. For settling, equate the magnitude 6πμaV_t to the net weight (4π/3)a³Δρg and cancel common factors to obtain the terminal formula. Thus the coefficient is derived from the explicit stress field, while the governing exterior solution itself is a stated classical input.

## Worked example: setup

Choose illustrative SI values a=10^(−5)m, μ=10^(−3)Pa·s, fluid density ρ_f=1000kg/m³, Δρ=1000kg/m³ and g=9.81m/s².

The formula gives V_t=0.000218m/s and Re=0.00436, small compared with one. Both expressions use exact terminating-decimal inputs, and the verifier evaluates them with rational arithmetic. The worked model explains the factor 6π, the direction of drag and the independent consistency check needed after calculating a speed.

## Limits

The derivation assumes an isolated sphere, no-slip boundary conditions and negligible inertia. Walls, finite Reynolds number, non-Newtonian rheology or slip change the problem. A small numerical Re supports this approximation within the declared model but is not a rigorous bound on every neglected physical correction.

## Extension

Within the same instantaneous-drag particle model, adding inertia gives mV′=F₀−6πμaV and an exponential relaxation time m/(6πμa). Unsteady fluid inertia and added-mass effects would require another model rather than being silently absorbed into that scalar ODE.
