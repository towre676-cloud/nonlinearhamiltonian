## Revised question retained in the annual source

Reconstruction: How are Hamilton’s equations related to the Hamilton–Jacobi PDE, how does a quadratic phase produce a Riccati equation, and why can that Riccati variable blow up while the mechanical trajectory remains smooth?

## Status

Classical mathematics newly developed from a recovered assistant-only catalog name

## Summary

Three separately named catalog items can be made concrete with one harmonic oscillator. The Hamiltonian flow is a smooth rotation in phase space; a quadratic Hamilton–Jacobi function describes a graph inside it; a scalar Riccati equation controls that graph’s slope. The finite-time pole is therefore a coordinate failure of the graph description, not a singularity in the underlying oscillator.

## Definitions

For a C² Hamiltonian H(q,p,t), canonical equations are qdot=H_p, pdot=−H_q. Along a solution, dH/dt=H_t.

A C² Hamilton–Jacobi function S(q,t) solves S_t+H(q,S_q,t)=0. Its momentum graph is p=S_q, on a region where this is a valid smooth graph.

For H=(p²+q²)/2 and the quadratic ansatz S=A(t)q²/2, the PDE reduces to the scalar Riccati equation A′+A²+1=0.

More generally y′=a(t)+b(t)y+c(t)y² is Riccati. If c is C¹ and nowhere zero, y=−u′/(cu) transforms it, wherever u≠0, into u″−(b+c′/c)u′+acu=0.

## Result name

Characteristic mechanics and an exactly solvable graph singularity

## Result statement

For the unit oscillator, q(t)=q₀cos t+p₀sin t and p(t)=p₀cos t−q₀sin t solve Hamilton’s equations for all real t. With initial graph p₀=0, the Hamilton–Jacobi solution S(q,t)=−(tan t)q²/2 is valid for |t|<π/2, and A(t)=−tan t solves the associated Riccati equation.

## Proof

The oscillator expressions differentiate to qdot=p and pdot=−q. Their squared norm is q₀²+p₀², proving energy conservation directly, while the general chain rule gives H_qH_p−H_pH_q+H_t=H_t. For a general smooth S solving Hamilton–Jacobi, follow qdot=H_p(q,S_q,t). Differentiating p=S_q along that path gives pdot=S_qt+S_qqH_p. Differentiating the PDE in q gives S_qt+H_q+H_pS_qq=0, so pdot=−H_q: its characteristics obey Hamilton’s equations. In the quadratic oscillator ansatz, S_t=A′q²/2 and H(q,S_q)=(A²+1)q²/2, yielding Riccati. The initial value A(0)=0 has A=−tan t, or equivalently A=u′/u with u=cos t solving u″+u=0. For the general substitution write v=u′/u. Then v′+v²=u″/u; substituting y=−v/c yields precisely the displayed linear second-order equation. These transformations are valid only while their denominators do not vanish.

## Worked example: setup

Set q₀=2,p₀=0 and evaluate at t=π/4, then inspect the limit t→π/2.

## Worked example: calculation

At π/4 the trajectory has q=√2,p=−√2 and energy two. The phase coefficient is A=−1, so p=Aq and S=−q²/2 agree with the same graph. The Hamilton–Jacobi residual is [A′+A²+1]q²/2=0 because A′=−sec²t. At π/2 the trajectory is (0,−2), perfectly finite, but A=−tan t diverges. All starting q₀ with p₀=0 project to q=0 there, so one graph p(q) can no longer represent the family. The verifier checks the analytic coefficient identities numerically and uses exact rational values for the A=−1 residual.

## Worked example: conclusion

The shared example explains both the strength and the domain of the Riccati reduction, including a genuine geometric reason for its pole.

## Limits

This is the classical Hamilton–Jacobi equation, not automatically the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation of an optimal-control problem. A local smooth phase need not exist globally after characteristics cross. The written calculation does not claim global smooth solutions for an arbitrary Hamiltonian.

## Extension

For a quadratic multidimensional Hamiltonian, a matrix phase S=qᵀPq/2 produces a matrix Riccati equation. The graph remains meaningful only while the relevant position block of the linear symplectic flow is invertible; a singular block again indicates a projection caustic.
