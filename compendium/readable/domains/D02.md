## Organizing question

Which geometric model, maps and comparison data make a claim about arithmetic, singularities or cohomology precise enough to prove?

## Overview

This domain is organized by the object to which a statement applies. An affine equation, its singular curve, the normalized function-field model and the smooth compact curve answer different questions. A point count, a rational group, a divisor class and a cohomology realization likewise retain different information. The best revision of the earlier questions is to specify that object first, then ask for a map or invariant with hypotheses that can be checked. This replaces a broad request for additional geometric structure with a sequence of concrete mathematical tasks.

The curve family begins with maps. Rational parametrization includes an exceptional point and an inverse domain. The cusp map t↦(t²,t³) is injective but has zero derivative at the origin; its normalization ring and local intersection multiplicity expose what a set-theoretic description misses. The 2026 corner cubic adds an exact birational coordinate change, while the superelliptic family computes genus only after finite and infinite ramification are counted on the smooth compact normalization. These examples provide reusable questions for any proposed parameter map: where is it defined, where does rank drop, what happens at infinity, and which inverse identity is actually proved?

Divisors and positivity organize the 2023 arithmetic-quotient cluster. Neatness addresses stabilizers in the open quotient; the cusp coordinate fills a puncture; a regular fan addresses local boundary smoothness; the canonical or log-canonical divisor addresses growth of sections; a very ample line bundle addresses an embedding. The modular curves X(3) and X(7) make ordinary and logarithmic general type visibly different. Riemann–Roch and Serre duality then give a precise degree threshold for a curve embedding. Picard calculations on P¹×P¹ and invariant divisor subspaces provide finite exact models of the same language. Higher-dimensional claims remain bounded by the actual compactification and positivity data supplied.

Elliptic arithmetic is the strongest local-to-global example. The discriminant-37 curve recurs in the 2024 and 2026 treatments with exact finite counts and rational group computations. Two good reductions exclude every possible rational torsion prime; the nonzero point then proves a rank lower bound. Heights turn independence into a Gram certificate, but a subgroup regulator differs by an index square from the full regulator. Selmer groups add hidden torsors and therefore supply information different from an explicit generator list. The interactive capacity here is specified as a recipe for exact point and group checks; no general certified rank or integral-point engine is claimed.

The cohomological family supplies a general caution about forgetting structure. An Euler realization can erase nonzero groups or take negative values. Rational monodromy ranks can erase integral torsion. A nilpotent operator determines a weight filtration but does not by itself produce a limiting mixed Hodge structure. Étale, crystalline, de Rham and Betti comparisons require compatible bases, coefficients and geometric hypotheses; a false universal motivic–Betti isomorphism already fails at a point. These are constructive constraints: they tell a future interface which fields and degrees to require and which counterexamples to expose. Together the four families form an atlas of models, maps and information loss, with each purported cross-domain identification stated as a theorem still requiring its own certificate.

## Open frontier

The next substantial work would supply explicit global compactification data in a fixed higher-dimensional example, certified descent and saturation for the elliptic subgroup, and correctly graded functorial comparison maps. A numerical invariant can assist those tasks but cannot replace the relevant geometry or cohomology.
