## Organizing question

What does the available observation distinguish, and what claim does its evidence actually justify?

## Overview

Information and inference begin with an observation map and a target. The map decides which states can be distinguished; the target and loss decide whether those distinctions matter. A sampling law then determines which statistical guarantees are available. This domain therefore organizes covariance, entropy, diagnostics and validation around a sequence of questions: what remains hidden, what transformation loses information, what a finite sample can establish, and what a summary preserves. These questions connect closely but their answers are not interchangeable.

Covariance geometry is the first layer. A covariance is a positive semidefinite quadratic form when second moments exist. Null directions record exact linear dependence, and positive averaging preserves the covariance cone. Nonlinear maps add a decisive qualification: a vanishing Jacobian can conceal a nonzero leading uncertainty at the next order. The squared-Gaussian example is fully calculable. It then becomes a hidden-target model, where observing one coordinate perfectly still leaves a squared hidden coordinate random. Conditional expectation gives the optimal estimator and an exact orthogonal decomposition of risk. This identifies separately the part due to structural invisibility and the part due to measurement noise.

Entropy and information geometry describe how laws compare. Relative entropy contracts under a specified stochastic channel; Shannon entropy increases under doubly stochastic mixing but can decrease on approach to a concentrated equilibrium. Mutual information is statistical dependence, not a causal arrow. In parameter space, local KL expansion produces the Fisher quadratic form, with a null direction for common softmax logits. These results explain what may be monitored during optimization while preserving the need to evaluate actual prediction loss independently. Matrix entropy has related exact identities, but the quantum algebra and positivity hypotheses remain visible.

Statistical inference adds sampling and selection. Identifiability is a statement about whether parameters produce distinct observation laws at the stated design. Calibration is a statement about a test statistic under a null. Generalization is a statement about unseen draws. A nested fit automatically improves training residuals, and a prescribed spectrum can always be embedded in a matrix; neither fact proves prediction. Finite uniform bounds and PAC–Bayes methods supply genuine guarantees under declared bounded-loss sampling models. Exact paired randomization supplies another route when the sign-exchangeability null is justified. Its finite p-value remains separate from effect magnitude and practical benefit.

The final family treats the capacity and limitations of summaries. Lossless reconstruction requires an injective encoding and therefore a counting lower bound. Prefix entropy grows by conditional information, independently of a chosen symbolic metric. Spectral flatness, circular concentration, AUROC and pooled correlation each retain particular features and discard others. Their precise invariances are useful; promoting one to a universal certificate is not. The resulting interactive direction is a panel of observation fibres, conditional-risk calculations and finite diagnostic populations. The current atlas supplies small verified fixtures and cross-links to observability and exact-counting labs, while general statistical estimation and historical empirical validation remain unclaimed.
