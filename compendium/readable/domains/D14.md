## Overview

The computation domain joins three questions that must remain distinct: what behavior a machine is intended to preserve, what smaller representation reproduces it, and what certificate establishes that conclusion. The observable state is relative to a declared action alphabet. Present outputs do not determine it. Future equivalence compares outputs after every allowed continuation, and the finite refinement theorem makes that infinite-looking condition executable on a supplied finite transition table. Additive costs enlarge the semantics and must descend with transitions. This explains why a state quotient can be exact for one observer and fail when a reset, query or pinned physical identity is added.

Linear realization gives the parallel quantitative theory of memory. An intertwining identity proves that a smaller state reproduces every time step, while a nonzero Hankel minor proves that no still smaller linear state suffices. The distinction between the reachable subspace and the invisible subspace matters: their intersection quotient determines zero-initial driven order. A recurrence for one observed trajectory, a universal scalar observation recurrence and a multi-output state dimension are different mathematical objects. The min–plus two-by-two gap illustrates the same upper-bound/lower-bound strategy in another algebra, with its scope explicitly limited to additive register factorization.

The certificate family supplies a common proof interface across the archive. Truth tables require a finite domain; quantifiers specify whether a witness is uniform; a polynomial function and a formal polynomial need not coincide; a feasible point is not an optimum; an exact witness is not a complete census. A small checker can validate useful discoveries made by an untrusted search procedure, but its conclusion depends on its declared certificate type. A Bézout identity, a rank factorization and a primal–dual pair establish different propositions. Historical proof diagrams, public recursion identities and two-prime checks retain their narrower meanings unless the missing completeness or dependency arguments are supplied.

The raw-generator theorem is a substantial instance where those obligations are actually met. The annual 2026 bundle contains all 126 integer lattice bases and exact determinant and inverse-column checks. They exclude every collision for any five known positions among nine raw steps, not merely the fixture states. A puncturing argument then proves exact distance n−4 and length-optimal one- and two-word correction. The decoder fixtures separately exercise the implementation. Accepted/rejected sequences have unknown hidden step counts and remain outside that theorem. This distinction should not obscure the genuine strength of the raw certificate.

The atlas’s interactive interface reflects this proof architecture. L01 makes a fixed linear quotient test and cheapest static probe panels inspectable; the finite-machine refinement and adaptive policies are checked recipes. L02 exposes a complete small census and address selection, while its completion-count and inverse-ranking theorems are proved separately. The broader exact certificates remain replayable source computations and mathematical recipes, rather than a claimed general theorem prover or universal SMT replacement. Their unification is a disciplined route from behavior to reduction to proof, with every change of model or quantifier visible.
