## Organizing question

What analytic representation makes the desired quantity computable without losing its domain, norm or normalization?

## Overview

This domain organizes the part of the archive concerned with representing an object so that a question about it becomes a controlled calculation. The representation may be a coefficient sequence, a contour integral, a special-function identity or a spectral measure. In every case, the first task is to state what is being represented and in which space. Bessel’s inequality sees an orthogonal projection; Parseval requires completeness. An atomic pairing uses cancellation in a particular measure. A Fourier transform needs a normalization, and a sampled FFT describes an interpolant before it describes any unknown continuous function. These distinctions provide constructive working rules, not merely objections to formulas. They show how to calculate the information retained by a representation and the error introduced by discarding the rest.

The first family develops that rule through transforms, frames and cancellation. Its four-coordinate Haar calculation accounts exactly for all energy and for the residual after discarding fine detail. A lower frame bound then converts a measurement discrepancy into a state discrepancy with an explicit constant. This is the analytical bridge to reconstruction, while the possibility of a nullspace explains why coefficient summaries can fail to identify an object. Weighted atoms and local Hardy–BMO estimates keep the same measure in the cancellation and pairing; directional image differences explain why a transform may be useful for an edge geometry without being a complete representation.

The second family puts local analytic certificates next to the global information they need. Cauchy extraction requires a holomorphic neighborhood; logarithmic derivatives record zeros or poles with multiplicity; contour counts do not distinguish two simple zeros from one double zero. Positive finite atomic measures give a particularly productive exact class. Their Herglotz functions have simple real poles, a fixed half-plane sign and one simple zero between each pair of poles. The proof uses only explicit signs and derivatives. Branch periods then demonstrate why locally closed differentials can still carry global continuation data.

The third family collects exact integration and special functions by method. Mellin moments, theta and modular reindexing, logarithmic integrals, dilogarithms, elliptic limits and finite trigonometric sums each retain their convergence and branch conditions. The elementary logarithmic moment supplies factorial weights and a rational tail bound for a zeta integral. Its role is to teach a reproducible reduction with a stopping rule, while the original member treatments retain the additional hypotheses needed for their richer constants.

The fourth family treats spectra as data of an operator with a domain and multiplicity. A diagonal model connects a counting function, a heat-trace exponent and a zeta half-plane. It separates fixed-time trace class from a singular short-time limit and shows why changing multiplicity changes dimension. Together, the four families suggest a practical order: fix the representation and its information loss, establish the analytic certificate, calculate with controlled tails, and only then interpret a spectral or geometric invariant. The progression entries identify dated source topics across 2023–2026; they do not date the reconstructed proofs or assert historical discovery priority.
